## Value chain and where a small trader fits

## Certification, paperwork, and what makes a part sellable

A part is sellable when a serious buyer can answer five questions with evidence: **what is it, where did it come from, what is its life/condition, who released it, and is it eligible for this aircraft/jurisdiction**. FAA Form **8130-3** is the U.S. Authorized Release Certificate/Airworthiness Approval Tag, and the FAA uses it for production and maintenance release contexts. EASA **Form 1** is the equivalent release document in EASA’s system. For parts returned to service from FAA-certificated sources, the FAA’s own unapproved-parts guidance says the accompanying records may include 8130-3, EASA Form 1, repair-station record entry, or air-carrier maintenance entry, depending on context. **Confidence: established.** citeturn7search2turn7search3turn7search10turn38search2

Who can issue these tags matters. New-production release comes from authorized production entities and designees under Part 21. Used-component return-to-service release comes from appropriately approved maintenance organizations, such as FAA Part 145 repair stations or EASA Part-145 organizations. EASA states that release is issued under the maintenance organization approval reference, and for transatlantic acceptance of certain used components, **dual release** under the FAA/EASA bilateral framework is often required. A **broker cannot lawfully self-issue an 8130-3 or Form 1** merely because he sourced the part. **Confidence: established.** citeturn7search1turn7search8turn32search0turn32search2turn32search3turn32search1

For **life-limited parts**, the regulatory minimum is more nuanced than the market slogan “back-to-birth” suggests. EASA explicitly says the term **“back to birth” is not used in European regulations**. IATA’s LLP guidance similarly notes that the FAA Chief Counsel has opined that full back-to-birth is not legally required by regulation in all cases. But that is not the same as saying it is commercially optional: IATA and industry guidance make clear that LLP transactions in practice often require near-complete life-status and installation/removal history, especially where lessors or sophisticated buyers are involved. In other words, regulatory sufficiency and market sufficiency are not the same thing. **Confidence: established.** citeturn6search1turn6search2turn6search15

Condition codes are widely used but not always legally defined. ASA has explicitly noted that terms such as **Factory New** and **New Surplus** are common commercial descriptors and do not have regulatory definitions in the way many buyers assume. In normal market usage, **NE** means new, **NS** new surplus, **OH** overhauled, **SV** serviceable, and **AR** as removed; these codes are commercially useful, but the supporting documents matter more than the letters. A “SV” unit with clean trace and valid release paperwork is a marketable product; an “SV” unit described only in an email is a problem. **Confidence: established.** citeturn31search2turn31search4turn31search0turn31search15

A broker without a repair station remains fully legal if he stays in his lane. The FAA has long said it **does not certificate or regulate aircraft parts distributors** in the same way it regulates certificate holders. But the broker is still exposed under **14 CFR 3.5**, which prohibits fraudulent or intentionally misleading statements about airworthiness or installation eligibility in advertisements and sales transactions. That means your legal operating model is: buy/sell, market, coordinate, verify documents, and disclose condition accurately—but never pretend that you are the releasing authority. **Confidence: established.** citeturn8search0turn38search1turn38search6

On accreditation, **ASA-100 / AC 00-56B** is voluntary, not mandatory. The FAA strongly endorses participation, and the ASA-100 accredited-company register is active in 2026. Early on, I would not delay market entry waiting for ASA-100 if you are doing pure brokerage with no physical inventory handling. I would, however, build your internal QMS **as if** you were going to be audited: receiving checklist, records control, quarantine procedure, tag verification, trace matrix, sanctions screening log, and supplier approval file. Then pursue accreditation once you have initial fit, especially before scaling consignment or stocking. **Confidence: emerging.** citeturn24view5turn8search1turn8search13turn39search1turn39search17

**So what for a small entrant:** your product is not “parts.” Your product is **trusted paperwork plus fast execution**. If you master records, applicability, release logic, and honest condition language, you can trade legally without owning a repair station. If you get loose with tags, trace, or condition claims, you are dead.

## Cross-border compliance and the things you must never do

## Go-to-market, regional demand, and the path to the first ten thousand in gross profit

## Recommended model, action plan, ranked risks, and first contacts
