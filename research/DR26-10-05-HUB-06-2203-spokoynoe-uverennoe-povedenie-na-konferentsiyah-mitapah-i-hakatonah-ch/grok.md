I'll verify the load-bearing studies and effect sizes before writing the brief.## BLOCK 1 - The mechanism: why a competent adult freezes in a room of strangers

1. **What is firing is social-evaluative threat, not a character hole.** The lab version is the Trier Social Stress Test (Kirschbaum, Pirke, and Hellhammer, 1993, *Neuropsychobiology*, https://doi.org/10.1159/000119004). Procedure: a few minutes to prepare, then a speech to a panel that gives you no encouragement, a camera running, then mental arithmetic out loud, and if you stumble they send you back to the start. Competent adults show a real cortisol and heart-rate rise. **SINGLE STUDY as a protocol, used in hundreds of later experiments.**

2. **The ingredient that drives the hormone response is being judged, plus not being able to control the outcome.** Dickerson and Kemeny (2004) pooled 208 acute-stress lab studies (*Psychological Bulletin*, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.355). Tasks with social-evaluative threat (an audience, a comparison, or a recording) produced a cortisol effect of **d = 0.67**. Tasks where nobody could judge the performance produced **d = 0.15**. Uncontrollable judged performance was the worst combination. **REPLICATED (meta-analysis).** A room of strangers who might hire you, fund you, or remember a clumsy sentence is a messy, weaker version of that task. Your body is running the right program for the wrong century.

3. **You overestimate how much of you they can see. Two separate errors.**
   - **Spotlight effect.** Gilovich, Medvec, and Savitsky (2000), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 78, 211–222. Paper text: people who walked in wearing an embarrassing T-shirt estimated notice at **exactly twice** the rate observers actually achieved (https://web.archive.org/web/20180819214359/https://msu.edu/course/psy/101/altmann/GilovichMedvecSavitsky00.pdf). Accounts of Study 1 put the raw split near **46% estimated versus 23% actual**. Study 3: in a group discussion, people also overestimated how much their own remarks stood out, good remarks and bad remarks both. **SINGLE STUDY paper, several experiments, finding direction repeatedly shown.** A stumbled word is less visible than it feels. A brilliant line is also less visible than it feels.
   - **Illusion of transparency.** You think the anxiety is leaking. Savitsky and Gilovich (2003), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103103000568). Speakers felt nervous at **M = 5.95**, thought they looked nervous at **M = 6.55**, and the other person rated them at **M = 5.25** (0–10). About **1.3 points** of "they can see me shaking" was fiction. In Study 2, people told the specific fact (your nerves show less than you think) were rated higher on speech quality by observers (**4.23**) than a control group (**3.50**) and higher than a group told only the vague line "it's normal, relax" (**3.62**). Vague reassurance did not do the job. The specific bias did. **SINGLE STUDY paper.**

4. **Liking gap. They like you more than you think they did.** Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom, and Clark (2018), *Psychological Science*, 29, 1742–1756 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30183512/, https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714). After conversations, people systematically underestimated how much the partner liked them and enjoyed them. In the lab acquaintance data, actual liking was **M = 5.82**, and perceived liking sat reliably lower (**b = −0.65**, p < .001). The gap showed up in short, medium, and long talks, in a UK adult workshop, and in college dormmates tracked for months. Part of the cause: people grade their own conversational performance more harshly than the tape deserves. **SINGLE STUDY paper, five contexts, one lab group. Not a multi-lab meta-analysis.** It has not, in what I could find, been buried by a failed replication.

5. **Beautiful mess effect. Your vulnerability looks worse to you than to them. Boundary matters.** Bruk, Scholl, and Bless (2018), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 115, 192–205, seven studies (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30024240/). Asking for help, admitting a mistake, showing an imperfection: observers rated it more kindly than the person doing it. Author summary: https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/other-people-dont-think-youre-a-mess/. **SINGLE STUDY paper, seven experiments.**
   **Boundary, do not ignore this at a working dinner.** Gibson, Harari, and Marr (2018), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597815302521). Three experiments: when a **higher-status** person disclosed a weakness during a task, receivers lowered that person's status, influence, and relationship quality. Peer-status disclosers did not take that hit. So: a one-sentence admission of a bug, after you are already a peer in the conversation, matches the beautiful-mess finding. Opening a VC table with "I am terrified and also not the strongest coder here" while you are trying to be taken seriously matches the status-penalty finding.

6. **Why online fluency does not transfer.** The mechanical difference is editability and undo, described by Walther's hyperpersonal model (Walther, 1996, *Communication Research*; https://doi.org/10.1177/009365096023001001). Online you get composition time, a backspace, asynchronous replies, and a tab you can close. The room deletes all four. Status is read live off latency (how fast you answer), gaze, and whether your sentence arrives intact. There is no draft. The same man can write a thousand public messages and still freeze, because those are different tasks with different threat math. **SINGLE STUDY as a theory paper, heavily used since. The channel difference itself is the claim to keep.**

7. **Accent and non-native speech. The penalty is real, and it is mostly about their processing, not your worth.**
   - Foreign language anxiety is its own construct, separable from trait anxiety. Horwitz, Horwitz, and Cope (1986), *Modern Language Journal*, 70, 125–132 (https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/horowitz.pdf). In that classroom sample, **49%** agreed they panic when they must speak the foreign language without preparation, **33%** get nervous and confused while speaking, **28%** feel very self-conscious speaking it in front of other students. You are fluent, so you are not that student. The overlapping piece is "no preparation buffer." **SINGLE STUDY for those percentages.**
   - Accent stigma review: Gluszek and Dovidio (2010), *Personality and Social Psychology Review* (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20220208/). Non-native accent changes how listeners treat the speaker, on both sides of the conversation. **REPLICATED as a literature review, not one experiment.**
   - Credibility hit from processing difficulty, not only from prejudice: Lev-Ari and Keysar (2010), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 46, 1093–1096 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103110001459). Identical trivia was rated less true in a non-native accent. On their scale, means were about **7.59 native, 6.95 mild accent, 6.84 heavy accent**. When listeners were told that any difficulty was just the accent, the **mild-accent penalty went away** and the **heavy-accent penalty stayed**. A fluent-but-accented speaker sits nearer the mild case. The lever is easier sentences, not a new accent. **SINGLE STUDY.**
   - Speech rate is curvilinear, not "slower is better." Munro and Derwing (2001), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 23, 451–468 (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/modeling-perceptions-of-the-accentedness-and-comprehensibility-of-l2-speech-the-role-of-speaking-rate/E22C75E244694BF6D6E083149FBEFA51). Listeners gave the best accentedness and comprehensibility ratings to L2 speech that was **somewhat faster than the rate L2 speakers usually adopt**. Very fast and very slow were both rated worse. They showed this by digitally speeding and slowing the same utterances, so it was the rate, not hidden proficiency. **SINGLE STUDY, two experiments.** Do not crawl. Do not race. Use your ordinary rate, and put the pauses at the ends of clauses, not in the middle of words.
   - First-sentence structure that reduces their load. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT, built on the fluency finding above.** Put the point in the first eight words. Then stop. Then the detail. Template, say it as three short sentences: "I build open-source agent tools. One person, many machines. I want to see where they break." Stress the content words: build, open-source, break. Do not spend sentence one on "sorry about my accent." I found no trial showing that apology improves comprehension. It spends the fluent window on you. **UNSOURCED as a ban; the positive instruction (short first sentence) follows Lev-Ari.**

### ANTI-SECTION - five popular explanations that do not survive

8. **"You lack confidence."** Confidence is the aftertaste of evidence. The TSST spikes competent adults who are not timid people. The input is a judged, uncontrollable performance. Treating it as a missing personality trait sends you to affirmations instead of repetitions. **The threat account is REPLICATED. The confidence-deficit account is UNSOURCED as a mechanism for this specific split (fluent online, frozen in the room).**

9. **"Fake it till you make it."** The famous bodily version (power pose) failed on hormones and behavior. See Block 2. The one tested sentence that does something ("I am excited") changes feelings more reliably than observer-rated performance, and the performance piece failed a 2025 direct replication. Pretending you are calm fights the arousal instead of using it. **The slogan is FAILED as a general fix.**

10. **"It is low testosterone."** The cortisol gap in Dickerson and Kemeny is between situations (audience versus no audience), not between men who have "enough T" and men who do not. Power-pose attempts to move testosterone failed (Block 2). Nothing in that literature explains a man who can talk for hours on a call and goes quiet in a lobby. The channel model does. **UNSOURCED as an explanation of this pattern, and the hormone shortcut has FAILED REPLICATION.**

11. **"You need to become an extrovert."** You already seek people for hours, in writing and on calls, every day. Extraversion is a preference for stimulation. Social-evaluative threat is a prediction that this room will grade you. Exposure updates the prediction. It does not install a new trait. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT against a popular frame; the maintenance model in Block 5 is the sourced alternative.**

12. **"Picture them naked," "just be yourself," "you think too much."** No trial supports the naked trick. "Be yourself" is not a procedure. "Think less" misses the content of the thought. The thoughts are specific and biased in known directions: they can see the nerves (they mostly cannot; Savitsky, about 1.3 points), they liked you less than they did (liking gap), your accent made the content false (Lev-Ari, and it shrinks if the sentence is easy). The fix is a procedure for those three errors. **UNSOURCED folklore.**

## BLOCK 2 - The 24 hours and the 10 minutes before the door

13. **Slow breathing. What is actually supported.** Zaccaro and colleagues (2018), systematic review, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6137615/). Slow breathing under **10 breaths per minute** is linked to higher heart-rate variability and to lower self-reported anxiety, arousal, and confusion in healthy adults. The physiological reason: a longer exhale raises parasympathetic (vagal) influence on the heart via respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and the slower rate is the ingredient the review could defend. **REPLICATED as a review of slow breathing. Not a trial of one magic ratio.**
    Door dose, exact: **inhale 4 seconds, exhale 8 seconds, 6 cycles.** That is a 12-second cycle, 5 breaths per minute, about 72 seconds. The 4/8 split itself is a practical way to land inside the reviewed band. It is not a branded protocol with its own effect size. Do not use "4-7-8" as if that brand had a trial. It does not, in anything I could source.

14. **The physiological sigh, exact procedure, exact limit of the evidence.** Balban, Neri, Kogon, Spiegel, Huberman and colleagues (2023), *Cell Reports Medicine* (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/). Cyclic sighing: **inhale through the nose until the lungs are full, take a second short inhale to pack them, then exhale slowly through the mouth until empty.** Their RCT: **111 people, 5 minutes a day, 28 days**, versus mindfulness and versus two other breath patterns. All groups improved. Breathwork beat mindfulness on mood. Positive affect rose about **1.91 points** on PANAS in the breathwork arms versus **1.22** in mindfulness (roughly a third more; Stanford summary: https://med.stanford.edu/news/insights/2023/02/cyclic-sighing-can-help-breathe-away-anxiety.html). Cyclic sighing was the best of the three on daily positive mood and it lowered resting breathing rate across the day. **SINGLE RCT.** It did **not** show that three sighs at a door halve state anxiety. A 2-minute door version is the same movement, at a dose they did not test. Use it anyway because the cost is zero. Do not tell yourself it deleted the fear.

15. **Cold.** A brief cold face splash (water on the cheeks and closed eyes, 15–30 seconds) triggers the mammalian dive reflex: heart rate drops. That physiology is real. I do not have a social-anxiety RCT showing it changes behavior at a party. An ice bath before a dinner is a bad trade (wet, shivering, another thing to manage). **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT for a 15-second face splash only. Skip if it makes you monitor your body harder.**

16. **Caffeine.** McLellan, Caldwell, and Lieberman (2016), review, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763416300690). Low to moderate doses (about **40–300 mg**, roughly a small coffee up to a strong one) improve alertness and vigilance. High doses raise tension, nervousness, and anxiety. Half-life in healthy adults is on the order of **5 hours** (range roughly 3–7). Exact rule: **one coffee, finished 60–90 minutes before you walk in. None after dinner if tomorrow is another event.** A second espresso at 9 p.m. buys jitter now and a worse amygdala tomorrow (item 18). **REPLICATED for the dose pattern. The 60–90 minute clock is PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT on top of the half-life.**

17. **Food.** Arrive fed, not stuffed. A normal meal about 2 hours before. Do not walk in fasted (you will drink faster and your hands will already be unsteady). Do not eat a heavy plate in the 60 minutes before a talk. **UNSOURCED - my judgement.** The "glucose restores willpower" story failed replication years ago. I am not using it.

18. **Sleep is the highest-value item in the 24 hours.** Yoo, Gujar, Hu, Jolesz, and Walker (2007), *Current Biology* (https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0960-9822(07)01783-6). About **35 hours** awake produced a **+60%** amygdala response to negative images versus slept controls, with weaker prefrontal coupling. One bad night turns the volume up on threat. Protect the night before a high-stakes room as if it were part of the event. **SINGLE STUDY, widely cited, specific to emotional pictures rather than parties. The direction is the part to trust.**

19. **Alcohol is a bad tool for this job.** Steele and Josephs (1990), *American Psychologist*, alcohol myopia (https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.45.8.921). Alcohol narrows attention onto whatever cue is loudest and blocks the conflicting thought. It does not install social skill. In you, the loud cues at a dinner are "pitch all three goals now" and "leave." Both get amplified. Anxiety relief in their account shows up when attention is also distracted, not as a clean calm. Next-day rebound anxiety is widely reported; **I do not have a clean effect size in hand (UNSOURCED magnitude).** What I can defend: alcohol fragments sleep, and sleep loss has a measured threat effect (item 18). **Rule: none the day of a target event, none the night before a target event.** A drink after you are already in a good conversation, at a non-target night, is ordinary life. It is not the protocol.

20. **"I am excited." What Brooks actually did, and what survived.** Brooks (2014), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 143, 1144–1158 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24364682/). People about to sing karaoke, give a speech, or do math were told to say **"I am excited"** out loud, or to calm down, or to say "I am anxious." About **85%** of a separate sample thought "calm down" was the right advice. "I am excited" beat "calm down" on feeling more excited and, in the original, on performance (karaoke scores, rater scores of speeches, math). The reason she gives: anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal states, so the reappraisal does not have to drag you down to calm. It only has to relabel the arousal as approach. **SINGLE LAB, several tasks.**
    **Replication, 2025.** Poynter and Pasqualini, *Psychology of Language and Communication* (https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2026-64356-001). Direct replication of the speech study. Self-reported excitement replicated, **d = 0.486** versus Brooks's **d = 0.404** on that feeling. Anxiety itself did not fall. **Observer ratings of the speech did not differ.** Emotional Stroop did not differ. So: the sentence still moves the label you put on the arousal. The claim "this makes observers think you spoke better" has **FAILED one direct replication.** Keep the sentence. Do not expect the room to score you higher because of it.

21. **Implementation intentions.** Form: **"If [specific cue], then I will [specific action]."** Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) meta-analysis, 94 tests, more than 8,000 people, **d = 0.65** on goal achievement (full text mirror: https://archive.org/stream/06GollwitzerSheeranImplementationIntentionsAndGoalAchievement). A 2024 meta-analysis of **642 tests** found a smaller average, **d = 0.36**, with a range about **0.27 to 0.66** depending on outcome, and larger effects when the plan was if-then, the person actually wanted the goal, and the plan was rehearsed at least once (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563). **REPLICATED.** Say each plan out loud once in the car. Vague plans ("I'll be more social") do not get the effect.

    Five plans, written for these rooms:
    1. **Walking in alone.** If I enter and I do not know a face, then I pocket my phone, stand still for 30 seconds, and join the nearest group that has a gap.
    2. **The circle that will not open.** If I deliver one line and nobody turns a shoulder in, then I say "I'll let you continue" and I walk to the next group within 10 seconds.
    3. **"What do you do?"** If someone asks what I do, then I say only: "I build open-source tools so one person can run many AI agents," and then I ask what they are building.
    4. **Nobody has talked to me for 10 minutes.** If 10 minutes pass and I have not spoken, then I ask the nearest person one question: "Which session today was actually worth it?"
    5. **Wanting to leave early.** If I want to leave before my planned time, then I stay for three more minutes and ask one follow-up question, and only then I may go.

22. **Third-person self-talk.** Kross and colleagues (2014), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, seven studies, **N = 585** (https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173; lab summary: https://lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/faculty-news/people-who-referred-to-themselves-by-name-during-stressful-self-.html). Using your name or "you," instead of "I," increased self-distancing. In the first-impression study and the speech study, the name/"you" group was rated better by observers, showed less distress, and (speech study) did less maladaptive post-event processing. A small meta-analysis inside the paper found **trait social anxiety did not moderate** the effect. It worked for the anxious participants too. **SINGLE LAB, seven studies.**
    Exact phrasing, in the car, once: **"Anton, you are not here to be impressive. You are here to ask two questions and stay three minutes."** Do not narrate your whole life in the third person out loud at the table. That reads as odd. Internal, or alone in the car.

### DEBUNKED in the pre-performance hour

23. **Power posing is dead as physiology and as behavior.** Carney, Cuddy, and Yap (2010) reported that two one-minute expansive poses raised testosterone, lowered cortisol, and raised risk-taking, N = 42. Ranehill and colleagues (2015), N = 200, experimenter blind, power over 95% to detect an effect that large (https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/dana_carney/ranehill.pdf). Felt power survived: mean difference **0.245** on their scale, **d = 0.34**, p = .017. Testosterone **d = −0.20**, p = .16. Cortisol **d = −0.16**, p = .27. Risk-taking **d = −0.18**, p = .22. All null, and the hormone signs went the wrong way. Simmons and Simonsohn's p-curve of the "supportive" literature judged the evidential value of the hormonal and behavioral claims as weak (*Psychological Science*, 2017). **FAILED REPLICATION for hormones and behavior. SINGLE surviving crumb: a small shift in felt power, which did not change what people did.** Standing like a starfish in a hotel bathroom will not change the room. Uncrossing your arms so you can gesture is fine, for a different reason (Block 3). Do it because your arms were in the way, not because of a hormone story.

24. **Visualizing success only.** Simulating the trophy (applause, the offer, the round) without simulating the moves is the weaker mental rehearsal. Process simulation (walking the steps) beats outcome simulation for getting the work done. I am not attaching an effect size because I did not re-pull the Pham and Taylor (1999) paper in this session. **Treat "close your eyes and see them clapping" as UNSOURCED for performance. See the if-then plans instead. Those have the meta-analysis.**

25. **"Picture the audience naked."** No trial. It adds a cognitive load right when you need working memory for their sentence. **UNSOURCED. Drop it.**

26. **Memorizing a script word for word.** This is a safety behavior (Block 5). You will lose one word, the chain breaks, and the catastrophe you were preventing arrives. Carry **three bullets**, not a text. For a speaker slot: one claim, one concrete example, one question you will ask the room. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT, consistent with the safety-behavior evidence.**

27. **Beta blockers without a doctor.** Propranolol can blunt tremor and racing heart in **situational** performance anxiety when a physician prescribes it. It is not first-line for social anxiety as a life pattern. Mayo-Wilson and colleagues (2014) put individual CBT first for social anxiety disorder, and SSRIs next if someone refuses therapy; see Block 5. Unsupervised use can be dangerous with asthma, low blood pressure, and some heart conditions, and it teaches your brain that you survived because of a pill. That is a safety signal. It blocks the learning. **Do not self-prescribe. The "this is how I become able to walk into rooms" plan is the wrong tool even when the drug is real.**

### The 10-minute door protocol

Do this in the car or the hallway. Phone goes away at step 4 and stays away.

28. **0:00.** Say out loud, once: **"I am excited."** Not "I am calm."
29. **0:20.** Say: **"Anton, ask two questions. Stay three minutes. Do not pitch."**
30. **0:40–2:40.** Eight cyclic sighs. Nose inhale, second short inhale, long mouth exhale. If a double inhale feels absurd, switch to inhale 4, exhale 8, six cycles.
31. **2:40.** Phone in the pocket, face down. Notes already written before you left the hotel.
32. **3:00.** Say the one if-then you are most likely to need tonight. Usually plan 2 or plan 5.
33. **3:30.** Say the identity sentence once, so your mouth has already made the sounds: **"I build open-source tools so one person can run many AI agents."**
34. **4:00.** Recall the prediction you wrote before leaving: "If I join one group, they will freeze me out. Test: stay three minutes and count how many actually answer."
35. **4:30–10:00.** Walk in at a normal pace. Stop inside the room for 30 seconds with empty hands. Find a group of three or more with a gap. Closed pairs (two people squared up, feet parallel) are the hard door. Skip them until you have one success. Join from the side.

## BLOCK 3 - The first 60 seconds and the conversation engine

36. **Opening a closed circle. Practitioner mechanics, not an RCT.** Taught in the same form by mingling practitioners (Jeanne Martinet, *The Art of Mingling*; Vanessa Van Edwards's conference material). I am not dressing this up as a trial. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.**
    1. Scan for a group of **three or more** whose feet leave a gap, or for one person standing alone. A closed pair is the lowest-percentage approach.
    2. Approach from the **side, about 45 degrees**, not through the middle like you are breaking a line. Stop at the edge, a bit more than an arm's length.
    3. Wait for a breath in their talk. Catch one person's eye. Say **one line that joins their topic.** Do not lead with your name plus your résumé.
    4. If a shoulder rotates toward you, step in. If it does not, you have about **10 seconds**. Then: "I'll let you continue." Leave. That attempt counts.
    5. Once in, your second sentence is a question about what they just said, not a pivot to you.

37. **The best-supported "be interesting" finding is follow-up questions.** Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson, and Gino (2017), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, "It Doesn't Hurt to Ask" (https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/asking-questions-increases-likability.html). Three studies: online getting-acquainted chats (600+ people) and face-to-face speed dates (110 people, 2,000+ conversations). People who asked more questions were liked more, **especially follow-up questions** (questions that stay on what the partner just said). The mechanism they measured was perceived responsiveness: you heard me. Switch questions (jumping to a new topic, which is what a pitch does) did not carry the same signal. In the speed-dating data, the popular write-up of their result: one extra question on each of 20 dates was associated with about **one additional "yes" for a second date.** A later critique (Kluger and Malloy, 2019) attacked the speed-dating analysis; the authors answered that the critique used the wrong variables and that all three studies still stand. Independent effect sizes cited by the critique were small, about **d = 0.35 and d = 0.27** in the two experiments. **SINGLE LAB, three studies, publicly contested on study 3, not a failed replication of the whole paper.** Small effect. Real enough to use, because the cost of asking one more follow-up is zero and the cost of pitching is high.
    Rule: a follow-up starts with their last noun. "You said the evals fall apart on the third hop. What does the third hop actually look like?" That is a follow-up. "Cool. So I built a fleet orchestrator" is a switch. Do not switch in the first minute.

38. **Twelve opening lines. No pitch in minute one.** These are tools, so the tag on all twelve is **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.**
    1. "Mind if I stand in? I walked in alone and your circle had a gap."
    2. "I caught the last ten words. What did they actually ship?"
    3. "Which session today was worth the walk?"
    4. "Are you building this week, or watching?"
    5. "What are you hoping to leave with, besides the stickers?"
    6. "I'm Anton. I don't know a face in this room. What pulled you in?"
    7. "You look like you've had this argument before. Which side is less wrong?"
    8. "Hackathon or the dinner? Which one are you actually here for?"
    9. "What is everyone here pretending is already solved?"
    10. "What breaks when you try to use agents on real work, not a demo?"
    11. "Who in this room will give me the unvarnished version?"
    12. "I'm going to the debate later. Are you speaking, or are you here to enjoy the wreck?"

39. **Ten follow-ups that keep a technical conversation alive and make them do the work.** **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT, shaped to match Huang's follow-up definition.**
    1. "What happened when you tried that?"
    2. "Where does it break?"
    3. "Who feels that, you or the user?"
    4. "What did you try before this?"
    5. "Say the part you are least sure about."
    6. "If it works, what changes Monday morning?"
    7. "Who told you that, and did you check?"
    8. "What would you stop doing if this actually worked?"
    9. "Walk me through the last time it failed."
    10. "What are you not saying because this room is full of investors?"

40. **Names, in a loud room. What is worth doing.**
    - Names are arbitrary tags, so they fall off faster than facts. The Baker/baker paradox (Cohen, 1990, *British Journal of Psychology*): the same word is remembered better as a job ("a baker") than as a surname ("Mr Baker"), because the job has associations and the name does not. **SINGLE STUDY pattern, classic, I did not re-pull a URL this session.**
    - Exact 4-step, all you can execute while a band is playing. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT on top of that paradox.**
      1. Repeat it immediately inside a real sentence: "Priya, you were in the Anthropic session."
      2. If you missed it, ask now: "Say it once more, it's loud." Guessing later is worse.
      3. Hook it to one visible thing or one fact they just said. One hook. Not a memory palace.
      4. Use it once more before you leave them. Within 60 seconds of stepping away, put "Priya / evals / Anthropic" in the phone. Review the list once before sleep. Not next week.

41. **Three goals, fifteen seconds. Pick one identity per conversation.** Recommendation, and it disagrees with "tell them everything so nothing is wasted." **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT, reasoned from Lev-Ari (processing difficulty lowers credibility) and from Huang (a switch into your agenda in minute one kills responsiveness).** Three missions in one breath force the listener to do extra work and force you into impression management, which is the self-focus Clark and Wells say keeps anxiety up. They cannot hire you, fund you, and adopt your repo in the same four sentences.
    The sentence, every time, until they pull another thread: **"I build open-source tools so one person can run many AI agents."**
    If they ask "are you looking?" then M2: "Yes. I want to be inside a lab, any level, as long as it is the real work."
    If they ask "are you raising?" or clearly run a fund, then M3: "Yes. US company, I need a technical co-founder and a first serious check. Not tonight's pitch. Can I send a page tomorrow?"
    If they build, stay on M1 and ask what breaks. Most people in these rooms are builders. Lead with the builder sentence. Let the room pull the other two. At a dinner that is explicitly a fundraise dinner, you may add the M3 sentence as sentence two. Still not a pitch deck.

42. **Status and body. Supported versus dead.**
    - **Eye contact, supported.** Binetti and colleagues (2016), *Royal Society Open Science*, about 500 people, 56 nationalities (https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/3/7/160086/36631/Pupil-dilation-as-an-index-of-preferred-mutual). Preferred direct-gaze duration **3.3 seconds** (mean 3295 ms). Almost everyone fell between **2 and 5 seconds**. Nobody wanted under 1 second or over 9. This was video clips, not a live party. **SINGLE large study.**
    - **What live conversation actually contains.** Rogers and colleagues (2018), *Scientific Reports* (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-22726-7). In 4-minute getting-acquainted talks, mutual **face** gaze was about **63%** of the conversation, in bursts of about **2.2 seconds**. True mutual **eye** contact was only about **12%**, in bursts of about **0.36 seconds**. People thought eye contact had been about **70%**. You are already doing more eye contact than you think, if you are looking at faces. **SINGLE STUDY.**
    - **Rule:** look at the face in bursts of about **2–3 seconds**, more while listening than while talking. Glance away to fetch a word. For a non-native speaker that glance is useful, because holding a face while searching for a word competes for the same effort. Come back when the word arrives. Do not stare. Do not aim at 100%.
    - **Hands, supported as communication, not as dominance.** Hostetter (2011), meta-analysis, 63 samples, *Psychological Bulletin* (https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022128). Gestures give listeners a **moderate** comprehension benefit. Larger when the gesture adds something the words do not fully carry (show "one person, many machines" with two hands). Larger for child listeners than adults, but the average effect is still there. **REPLICATED (meta-analysis).** Use gestures that draw the object. Do not wave for its own sake.
    - **Voice.** Rate: ordinary, not slow (Munro and Derwing, item 7). Volume: I have no sourced decibel target. **UNSOURCED - my judgement:** if they lean in, you are quiet; speak to the farthest of the three people. Pitch: do not perform a fake bass. It costs you consonant clarity, which is the thing Lev-Ari says you cannot afford. Stay at the bottom of your comfortable range. **UNSOURCED as a pitch prescription.**
    - **Taking up space: debunked as a hormone or behavior hack** (item 23). The only crumb is felt power, d = 0.34, with no behavior change. Uncross your arms so your hands can gesture. Then forget your body and listen.

## BLOCK 4 - Hackathons, demos, cameras, and speaking slots

43. **Hackathon, first 30 minutes.** The one sourced anchor is not a hackathon paper. Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi, and Malone (2010), *Science*, 330, 686–688, two studies, **699 people** (https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1193147). Group performance across tasks tracked a collective factor. That factor was **not** strongly tied to the average or the maximum individual intelligence. It **was** tied to average social sensitivity, to **how equally the talking was distributed**, and to the proportion of women in the group. The strongest coder dominating the air is, on this evidence, a tax on the team. **SINGLE paper, two studies, high impact. Not a hackathon field trial.**
    What to do with it. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT:**
    1. In the first 30 minutes, teams form around whoever names a role, not around whoever is best.
    2. Say this, verbatim: **"I'm not your strongest coder. I'm strong at cutting scope and keeping the demo alive. I'll take integration, the script, and the clock. You own the model. If that's useful, I'll stay."**
    3. That sentence claims a job. It does not claim rank. Gibson (item 5) is about weakness disclosures from someone sitting above the others. You are offering a peer role. Do not add a speech about your fear.
    4. Then run the Woolley behavior on purpose: every 15 minutes, ask the quietest person "what are you seeing?" and shut up.
    5. If they say no: "Good luck. I'll find another team." One sentence. Leave. The attempt is the rep. Do not bargain your way into a team that already closed.

44. **Demo failure. No RCT. This is the engineering practice that also matches the anxiety model.** **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.**
    1. Before you leave the hotel, record a **60-second** screen capture of the happy path on your phone. That file is the fallback.
    2. When live breaks, say exactly: **"Live just died. I won't debug it in front of you. Here is the same path from an hour ago."** Hit play within 5 seconds.
    3. Name the bug in one line, no apology paragraph: **"The API timed out."** A one-line admission is the beautiful-mess shape. A two-minute sorry is self-focused attention, which is the process that keeps the fear alive (Clark and Wells, Block 5).
    4. End with what they should remember: **"What I wanted you to see is one person driving four agents. That's the shot."**
    5. The failure is allowed to last **15 seconds**. Then you are back in the material.

45. **Camera fear, the old one, from 2017. Exposure plus a specific way of watching.**
    - Watching yourself **helps** only with preparation. Harvey, Clark, Ehlers, and Rapee (2000), *Behaviour Research and Therapy* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0005796799001485). Before the video, people (1) predicted in detail what they would see, (2) formed an image of it, (3) watched **as if watching a stranger**. That group rated their performance better afterward than people who just watched. A 2002 single-session follow-up found the same pattern and some generalization to a second speech. **SINGLE STUDY, replicated once in the same tradition.**
    - Watching yourself **hurts** when you do it the same night, hunting for proof you looked anxious. That is post-event processing with pictures (Block 5). **Rule: never watch the same night.**
    - "Talk to one person, not to the lens." **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT, tied to a real mechanism:** Clark and Wells's model says self-focused attention (watching yourself from the outside while you perform) maintains anxiety, and an external focus reduces it. A human face is an external target. A lens is a mirror. On a recorded call, look at the person. Let the camera sit off to the side.
    - "The second take is the take." **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.** Take one is a throwaway on purpose. You say, out loud, "this one is trash," and you do it. That violates the prediction that the recording itself is the verdict. Take two is the one you may keep. Do not chase take seven. Two is the protocol.
    - Camera ladder, solo, hotel or car. One rung a day until it is boring, then the next.
      1. Voice memo, 60 seconds, no video, one topic, in the car.
      2. Phone propped, lens pointed at the wall, you talk for 60 seconds, delete without watching.
      3. Lens at chest height, not your eyes, 60 seconds, delete without watching.
      4. Lens on your face, you talk to one person on a live call, local recording on. They are the audience.
      5. Next day only: write three sentences predicting how bad it looks. Watch once, as a stranger. Stop.
      6. Send one 45-second clip to one person who already knows you.
      7. Publish one 60-second clip, unedited, second take.

46. **Speaker slots. The fear curve.**
    - Behnke and Sawyer (1999), *Communication Education* (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03634529909379164). Anticipatory anxiety across a class speech was a **V shape**: **highest immediately before speaking**, second-highest when the assignment was first announced, **lowest while preparing.** The spike you want to obey is the peak, and the peak is at the door of the mic, not a reading of how the talk will feel.
    - Behnke and Sawyer (2004), *Communication Education* (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03634520410001682429). The jump from rest to the start, plus the change across the **first minute**, together accounted for **68%** of the variance in anxiety during the speech. Change during the **last minute added nothing.** For most speakers the worst internal moment is the start, and it turns during minute one if you keep going. **SINGLE LAB program of studies, not a meta-analysis. Use the shape, not the 68% as a personal guarantee.**
    - Short talk versus hovering at the edge. **No RCT shows talks beat mingling for getting hired. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT:** a 3-minute slot reverses the approach problem. After you speak, they have your name and a topic, so they open the circle. One talk, one identity (the tool). Hiring and the raise happen in the hallway after, when they walk to you. If-then: **"If I want to give the slot back in the ten minutes before, then I walk to the mic anyway, because that want is the peak of the curve, not information."**

47. **Q&A, exact words.** **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.**
    - You do not know: **"I don't know. What I do know is [one adjacent fact]. I'll send the number tomorrow."** Then stop. Do not invent. Do not add "sorry, my English."
    - Hostile: **"That's a fair hit. The weak point is [five words]. Here's what would change my mind."** Then: **"What would you accept as a test?"** Do not defend your whole life. Do not grin it away.
    - You half-heard it: **"I caught the first half and lost the rest in the room. Say it in one sentence?"** If still unclear: **"I want to answer the question you asked, not the one I guessed. Once more?"** You are managing their processing (Lev-Ari), not confessing a flaw.

## BLOCK 5 - Rejection, recovery, and keeping the muscle between events

48. **What makes exposure work. This is the best-evidenced block. Read it as rules, not inspiration.**
    - The big outcome number: Mayo-Wilson and colleagues (2014), network meta-analysis, *Lancet Psychiatry* (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4287862/). Versus waitlist, **individual CBT SMD −1.19** (credible interval −1.56 to −0.81). Group CBT **−0.92**. Exposure plus social skills **−0.86**. Self-help with support **−0.86**. **Self-help without support −0.75**. Individual CBT also beat psychological placebo (**−0.56**). Individual CBT was the best-supported first treatment. **REPLICATED (network meta-analysis).** These trials are on diagnosed social anxiety disorder. You may not meet that bar. Treat −1.19 as the size of the effect when the full problem is treated properly, not as your personal forecast. Solo work is not zero: unsupported self-help still beat waitlist at −0.75. It is smaller than guided CBT.
    - Older meta-analysis, same direction: Feske and Chambless (1995), *Behavior Therapy* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0005789405800401). Exposure-only and full CBT were similarly effective. More exposure sessions lined up with better social-anxiety scores at posttest. **REPLICATED (meta-analysis, 1995, smaller literature).**
    - Safety behaviors are why "I went to the party and nothing changed." Wells, Clark, Salkovskis, Ludgate, Hackmann, and Gelder (1995), *Behavior Therapy*, a within-subject series of **8 patients** (https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:c4f3815b-2524-40cd-9a39-80f645c25b45). One session of exposure alone versus one session of exposure **plus deliberately dropping safety behaviors.** Dropping them reduced in-situation anxiety and belief in the catastrophe more than exposure alone. **SINGLE STUDY, small series. The direction is the part the whole CBT tradition kept.**
    - Your likely safety behaviors, name them so you can drop them: phone in hand, rehearsed script, apologizing for your accent, standing against the wall, leaving at the first silence, scanning for the exit, alcohol as the entry ticket, over-preparing a talk until no word can be wrong, holding a glass with both hands so nobody sees a tremor.
    - Inhibitory learning, the modern rule set. Craske, Treanor, Conway, Zbozinek, and Vervliet (2014), *Behaviour Research and Therapy* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0005796714000606). Extinction is new learning ("I did the thing and the catastrophe did not happen"), not erasure of the old fear. Their optimization list: **expectancy violation, deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction, remove safety signals, variability, retrieval cues, multiple contexts, affect labeling.** The old rule "stay until anxiety falls 50%" is no longer the target. You can end the rep when the prediction has been tested, even if your heart is still up. **REPLICATED as a framework built on extinction research. Not every one of the eight tactics has its own social-anxiety RCT.**
    - Rules that make a rep count:
      1. Write the prediction before: "They will turn away within 5 seconds."
      2. Do the thing with the safety behavior **down**.
      3. Stay long enough to see the prediction win or lose. Three minutes is enough for a circle. You do not need to stay until you feel fine.
      4. One factual line after: what you predicted, what they actually did.
      5. Repeat the same rung until the prediction updates, then move up.
      6. Change the room. A skill that exists only at one café did not consolidate (multiple contexts).
      7. Do not rescue the rep with a drink, a friend you hide behind, or a script.

49. **A 10-rung ladder for this month in San Francisco. Solo. Each rung is a real rep only if the phone is pocketed.**
    1. Barista: order, then one extra sentence ("Busy morning?"). Eye contact about 3 seconds.
    2. Hotel desk: ask one unnecessary question ("Which way is quieter at night?") and wait for the whole answer.
    3. On the street: "Is this the line for the venue?" to one stranger. Stay for their answer.
    4. Badge line: "What brought you?" Four exchanges minimum.
    5. Walk into a room alone. Stand 60 seconds, no phone. Then join the nearest open group with line 1 from Block 3.
    6. Approach a group, deliver one line, stay 3 minutes past the urge to leave.
    7. Answer "what do you do?" with the one sentence. Ask a follow-up. No pitch.
    8. One specific question to a speaker or organizer about **their** work. Then stop talking.
    9. Hackathon: ask to join in the first 30 minutes using the role sentence in item 43.
    10. Lightning talk or live demo: 3 minutes, three bullets, fallback video already on the phone, second take if you are recording.

50. **Rejection therapy. Claim versus evidence versus the sane version.** Jia Jiang's *Rejection Proof* (2015) is a memoir of 100 days of odd requests, filmed. **Evidence status: N = 1, no control group, no clinical scale, stories selected after the fact. PRACTITIONER ANECDOTE.** The mechanism he bumped into is exposure with the catastrophe made small ("they say no, I am still alive"). The stunt version (humiliate yourself, film strangers, collect refusals for content) trains a different skill from being someone a lab wants to hire, and it drags in consent problems. **Sane version:** small real requests where "no" is an allowed, predicted outcome. "Mind if I join you for three minutes?" A no is a successful test of the prediction, and it counts on the card. Do not film it.

51. **The two hours after. This is where people waste the rep.**
    - Clark and Wells (1995), chapter in Heimberg, Liebowitz, Hope, and Schneier (eds.), *Social Phobia: Diagnosis, Assessment, and Treatment*. Four maintaining processes: an observer-perspective self-image, in-situation safety behaviors, anxiety-caused performance slips, and **anticipatory plus post-event processing.** After the event you run a post-mortem, reconstruct it as worse than observers saw, and that reconstruction becomes the memory you take next time. I am citing a book chapter; the model is the one the trials in item 48 are built on.
    - Review of the post-event processing studies: Brozovich and Heimberg (2008), *Clinical Psychology Review* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735808000196). PEP is repetitive, self-focused replay, and it also runs forward onto the next event. **REPLICATED as a reviewed phenomenon. The "how to break it tonight" steps below are the clinical translation, PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT where I go past the review.**
    - What breaks the loop, in order, in the car:
      1. Do not start the replay. First song, no analysis. The car is where you will do the damage.
      2. Then five lines, facts only, and stop. (a) How many approaches. (b) One sentence a specific person actually said. (c) The prediction and what happened. (d) Which safety behavior you used. (e) One name plus one fact. No adjectives about yourself. No "I was awkward."
      3. If the loop starts anyway, label it out loud: **"This is the post-mortem. It rewrites the tape."** Then one third-person line, Kross-style: **"Anton, you asked two questions and she answered. That is the tape."** Then the music goes back on.
      4. Do not watch any recording tonight (item 45).
      5. Do not text a play-by-play to anyone to get reassurance. Reassurance-seeking is another safety behavior. One factual line to a collaborator about a follow-up you owe is fine. "Was I weird?" is not.
    - Why this matters as much as the event: the liking gap and the illusion of transparency both say the raw tape was kinder than your review. PEP throws away the kinder tape and stores the cruel one. Next time you walk in already defeated by a fiction.

52. **The scorecard. Count attempts.** I do not have a social-anxiety RCT titled "count approaches, not business cards." **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT, tied to two sourced ideas:** implementation intentions work when they specify **your** response (Gollwitzer), and safety behaviors plus self-focus are what keep the fear in place (Wells; Clark and Wells). Outcome goals ("they liked me," "I got the intro," "I felt confident") put the score in their hands, so a quiet room becomes a verdict on you, and you skip the next one.
    Count, that night, only these:
    - Approaches (you spoke first).
    - Follow-up questions.
    - Times you stayed the extra three minutes.
    - Safety behaviors you actually dropped.
    - Predictions you tested, won or lost.
    Do not count cards, promises, mood, or whether you were interesting. A night with four approaches and zero follow-ups is a useful failure. A night with one approach and three follow-ups is a good night even if nobody hires you. Write the five numbers. Look at the row from last week, not at your feelings.

53. **Two events a week for months, without the crash.**
    - You currently pay three times: the day before, the hour of, and the two-hour trial afterward. Item 51 deletes the third bill. That is the main energy intervention. **PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT on the arithmetic, sourced on why the third bill exists.**
    - One event, one goal. Mixer: users of the tool. Dinner that is clearly capital: the co-founder or the check, and only after they have talked. Hackathon: the role. Rotating all three inside one night is how a 90-minute event costs you the next day.
    - Pre-commit the exit before you arrive: "At 90 minutes I thank one person and I leave." Leaving on a plan is not avoidance. Leaving because your chest tightened, at minute 20, is avoidance. The if-then in item 21.5 is the difference.
    - Sleep (item 18) and no alcohol the night before (item 19) are the recovery tools that have a mechanism. A "recovery day" of total isolation is fine only if you still hit rung 1 or 2. Craske's framework: long gaps with zero contact let the old prediction come back, and extinction that happened in only one room does not travel. **A barista sentence on the off day is the maintenance dose.**
    - Caffeine: one dose, early relative to bedtime (item 16). Two events on back-to-back nights means the second one is fought with a louder amygdala if you slept badly. Prefer a gap night if you can choose.
    - I will not give you a calorie, a supplement, or a "social fitness" heart-rate zone. **UNSOURCED and unnecessary.**

54. **The one-year view. What changes, and how long it takes.**
    - What changes is the **prediction**, not the personality. After enough violations ("I spoke and they did not eject me"), you still get a spike at the door. Behnke's curve says the spike at the start is normal. What changes is that you stop obeying it, and the replay afterward gets shorter because the stored tape is factual.
    - How long, from the trials rather than from slogans: the effects in Mayo-Wilson come from a **course**, typically on the order of a dozen sessions across a few months in the Clark and Heimberg protocols, not from one brave night and not from a single weekend. Feske and Chambless: more sessions, better posttest scores. I will not invent "it takes 47 exposures." **Anyone who gives you a precise exposure count for a non-diagnosed adult is guessing.**
    - Fears do not ride along for free. Party exposures will not fix the 2017 camera fear. Camera rungs fix the camera. Room rungs fix the room. Your phone and writing fear are already extinguished. Leave them alone.
    - Return of fear is expected if you stop (Craske: extinction is context-specific and time-sensitive). The one-year plan is not "graduate." It is two events a week while the season is hot, plus one tiny rung on ordinary days forever, plus one camera rung a week until publishing a one-minute clip is boring. Boring is the graduation criterion. Confidence is a side effect, and it arrives last.

7. Say one joining line. Ask about their last sentence.

9. Stay three minutes past the urge to leave.
10. If they do not open, say "I'll let you continue" and move.
11. In the car, write five facts. No adjectives. Then stop.
12. Score approaches and follow-ups. Ignore cards and mood.

## C. WHAT I WOULD DO IF I ONLY HAD 3 THINGS

55. **First: graded exposure with the safety behaviors named and dropped, scored as attempts.** This is the only item here with a large clinical effect (Mayo-Wilson, individual CBT about **−1.19** versus waitlist; exposure-based classes around **−0.86**; even solo self-help about **−0.75**). Wells's small series says the rep barely counts if you keep the phone, the script, the apology, and the early exit. Breathing, posture, and "I am excited" do not update the prediction "if I speak, I will be rejected." Reps do. The popular answer, "get confident and then go," reverses the order the evidence supports. The feeling follows the violated prediction. It does not precede it.

56. **Second: kill the post-mortem the same night, every time.** Liking-gap and illusion-of-transparency data say the tape was kinder than you will remember if you replay it. Post-event processing (Clark and Wells; Brozovich and Heimberg) writes the cruel version into memory and hands it to you at the next door. People "face their fears" for years and stay stuck because they expose at 8 p.m. and un-expose from 9 to 11. A five-line factual card plus a hard stop is the difference between a rep and a scar. This beats any pre-game ritual, because the ritual wears off at the door and the stored tape does not.

57. **Third: one identity sentence, then follow-up questions, no pitch in minute one.** Huang's finding is small (around d = 0.3) and from one lab, and a 2019 critique poked the speed-dating piece. I still rank it above a polished three-goal pitch, because Lev-Ari shows extra processing load makes you sound less credible, and because stuffing hiring, fundraising, and the repo into the first breath maximizes self-focus on the exact night your threat system is already hot. Your online skill is already "talk about the work and ask what breaks." The room skill is to stop editing and keep that habit for three minutes.

    I am deliberately **not** ranking breathing, cold water, or "I am excited" in the top three. Use them. They are on the card. Brooks's performance claim already failed one direct replication, and the sighing RCT is a month of mood practice, not a social-performance trial. They are state shifters. They are not the thing that retires a fear documented since 2017.

## D. WHERE I MIGHT BE WRONG

58. **I framed the freeze as social-evaluative threat in the Clark and Wells pattern.** That fits "fluent online, frozen when I can be seen, then a cruel replay." It is the wrong frame if the real driver is sensory overload in loud rooms, auditory processing when several native speakers talk at once, or a specific bad event I do not have. Overturn: a proper assessment showing the trigger is noise and language load rather than being judged, or a history in which the fear started from one incident and stays tied to that incident. The accent-in-noise problem may be doing more work than shame. If so, the ladder still helps, but the first intervention becomes "move the conversation three meters away from the speaker" and shorter sentences, not more courage talk.

59. **I left "I am excited" on the wallet card after a failed performance replication.** Poynter and Pasqualini (2025) kept the feeling and lost the observer effect. A second failure, or a test in non-native speakers where the sentence adds a monitoring burden and makes speech worse, would pull it off the card. It stays only because it is one second long and the self-report piece replicated once. It is not load-bearing. If you drop everything except the three items in section C, drop this too.

60. **I told you to hide two of the three goals.** Huang measured liking, not job offers and not term sheets. A VC dinner exists so people can hear an ask. I may be optimizing for being liked in a room where being slightly blunt, after two minutes of listening, outperforms being mysterious. Overturn: a field test at these exact events where people who state the ask in sentence two get more next-day meetings than people who only ask follow-ups. The correction, if I am wrong, is narrow: keep the follow-up rule for minute one, and allow the M3 sentence as sentence two **only** when the other person is obviously there to fund or to be a co-founder. Do not "correct" it by going back to a three-goal monologue. That version loses on processing fluency even if I am wrong about timing.
