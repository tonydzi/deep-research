# BLOCK 1 — The mechanism

1. **Social-evaluative threat.** Situations where you can be negatively judged by others produce the strongest cortisol responses in the lab. The Trier Social Stress Test (public speaking + mental arithmetic in front of an unresponsive panel) is the standard paradigm and reliably triggers cortisol increase in ~70% of participants. REPLICATED. Kirschbaum, Pirke & Hellhammer (1993), "The 'Trier Social Stress Test'", *Neuropsychobiology* 28:76–81. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8255413/ Room of strangers + VC evaluators = a mild Trier. Your body is doing what it is built to do.

2. **Spotlight effect.** Observers notice your gaffes far less than you believe. Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky (2000): participants wearing an embarrassing T-shirt estimated ~46% of observers would notice; actual ~23% — roughly a 2x overestimate. REPLICATED. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11166022/ Practical read: whatever you did wrong, half as many people saw it as you think.

3. **Illusion of transparency.** We overestimate how visible our internal states (nervousness) are. Gilovich, Savitsky & Medvec (1998): nervous speakers estimated observers saw far more anxiety than observers actually reported. SINGLE STUDY (widely cited, conceptually replicated). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9740881/ Your pounding heart is not leaking. People literally cannot see it.

4. **Liking gap.** After brief conversations, people systematically rate how much the other person liked them *lower* than the other person actually liked them. Boothby et al. (2018), "The liking gap in conversations", *Psychological Science* 29(11). REPLICATED (study series; conceptually replicated in 2021 follow-ups). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30259007/ In the room, assume they liked you more than your brain reports.

5. **Beautiful mess effect.** Others rate your admissions of vulnerability (nervousness, failure) more positively than you rate your own. Bruk, Scholl & Mayer (2018), *JPSP* 115(2), self-verification theory explains the reversal. SINGLE STUDY (multi-experiment). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29952625/ "I'm a bit jet-lagged and this is my third event today" makes you *more* likable, not less.

6. **Why online fluency doesn't transfer.** Mechanically: (a) no edit buffer — text has seconds of latency, live speech has zero; (b) no exit — you can't close a tab mid-sentence; (c) real-time status reads — faces give continuous evaluation signals, text does not; (d) one-to-many audience ambiguity vs one known reader. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT synthesizing the social-evaluative-threat literature above — no single paper compares these directly. UNSOURCED - my judgement (the components, spotlight/transparency/liking, are sourced above).

7. **Accent / non-native anxiety.** Foreign language anxiety is a documented construct (Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope, 1986 — the FLCAS scale, thousands of citations): speaking anxiety is highest about *evaluation*, not vocabulary. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24221718/ (Horwitz 2001 review). What reduces listener friction: slower speech rate and clear pausing are the best-supported practical levers — accented speech at a slower rate is rated as intelligible as faster native-like speech; single-word findings summarized in phonetics literature (e.g., work on intelligibility and rate, Anderson-Hsieh & Venkatagiri, *ELT Journal* 1994). PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT for the operational advice: drop rate ~15%, pause at every sentence boundary, front-load a short prepared first sentence (this removes the highest-anxiety moment — the opening — which is also where listeners form their first "accent cost" judgment). An accent after the first 30 seconds is treated as noise by listeners; the first 30 seconds is where it costs you.

**ANTI-SECTION — popular explanations that are wrong or unsupported:**
1. "You lack confidence" — circular, unfalsifiable, predicts nothing.
2. "Fake it till you make it" — no evidence it reduces state anxiety in-the-moment; self-incongruence can *increase* it (imposter-lit., Clance & Imes 1978, originally clinical observations). PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT on the debunk.
3. "It's low testosterone" — testosterone status is not causally linked to momentary social freezing in normal-range men; unsupported for this use case.
4. "You need to be an extrovert" — introversion/extraversion predicts frequency of socializing, not ability to perform one conversation; socially anxious introverts improve with practice at the same rate. UNSOURCED - my judgement (the extraversion-performance link is weak; I know of no direct trial).
5. "You have a fixed social skill deficit" — social anxiety research shows the *performance* deficit is largely state anxiety, not skill: anxious people perform near-normally when anxiety is reduced (e.g., feedback studies in Clark & Wells' model, 1995). https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:339091/FULLTEXT02 — see also Stopa & Clark (1993), *Behaviour Research and Therapy*.

# BLOCK 2 — 24 hours and 10 minutes before

1. **Extended-exhale breathing.** Slow breathing with exhale longer than inhale increases vagal (parasympathetic) tone; ~6 breaths/min with an extended expiration is the classic protocol. REPLICATED (multiple HRV studies; e.g., Lehrer & Gevirtz (2014) review of HRV biofeedback, *Frontiers in Psychology*). https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00780/full **Exact procedure: inhale 4s through nose, exhale 6–8s through mouth, 6 cycles ≈ 1 minute.** Repeat 3 rounds at the car.

2. **Physiological sigh.** Double inhale (one full, one short top-up) through the nose, then long full exhale through the mouth. This offloads CO₂ rapidly and reduces subjective stress and arousal in real time; shown in humans (Balban et al. 2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*: cyclic sighing 5 min/day outperformed mindfulness for mood and reduced respiratory rate). SINGLE STUDY for that trial; the respiratory mechanism is textbook physiology. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36708660/ **Do 2 physiological sighs at the door.**

3. **Caffeine.** Caffeine amplifies state anxiety and physiological arousal (multi-study; caffeine challenge reliably raises anxiety scores, e.g., Hjorthø et al. and classic Linköping caffeine-anxiety work; a common cited result is that ~400mg raises anxiety in ~most sensitive individuals). UNSOURCED - my judgement for the exact mg framing. Rule: no caffeine within 3h of walking in; if you need it, half your usual dose, >3h before.

4. **Alcohol.** Acute low-dose alcohol reduces subjective social anxiety (real anxiolytic effect), but tolerance develops within days of regular use, and rebound anxiety + sleep disruption follow evening drinking. REPLICATED (social anxiety and alcohol literature; e.g., Carrigan & Randall 2003 review, *Clinical Psychology Review*). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12798107/ One drink max at events where you speak; zero the night before a speaker slot.

5. **Sleep/food.** One bad night measurably raises next-day anxiety reactivity (sleep restriction raises amygdala reactivity — Yoo et al. 2007, sleep-deprived vs rested amygdala responses). SINGLE STUDY (famous, neuroimaging). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17475757/ Protein + slow carbs ~2h before; don't enter a room hungry (hunger amplifies threat sensitivity). PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT on the food line.

6. **Cold exposure.** Cold showers for in-the-moment social anxiety: no direct trials; the arousal-reduction logic (sympathetic reset) is PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT. Not banned, just not evidenced.

7. **Anxiety reappraisal — "Get excited."** Brooks (2014), "Get excited: reappraising pre-performance anxiety as excitement", *JEP: General* 143(3). Participants said "I am excited" (out loud) before singing/speaking/math; the reappraisal condition outperformed "calm" and control on performance and rated themselves more excited. Effect: significant performance improvement across 3 experiments; the core mechanism — anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal, only the valence label differs, and relabeling is easier than down-regulating arousal. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24661156/ Replication status: the "anxiety→excitement" relabel has replicated conceptually in several performance domains; arousal-reappraisal research broadly is REPLICATED, exact wording effects vary. **Exact self-talk sentence: "I am excited."** Said out loud. Not "calm down" — calming is the harder physiological move.

8. **Implementation intentions (Gollwitzer).** If-then plans roughly double goal attainment across ~100 studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*; 94 studies, medium-to-large effect d≈.65). REPLICATED. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0065260106000024 Format: IF [situation], THEN [exact action]. Five for him:
   1. IF I walk into a room alone, THEN I stand still for 10 seconds, scan for a group of two people with open shoulders (not a closed circle), and walk to them within 30 seconds.
   2. IF the circle won't open, THEN I stand at the edge, make eye contact with one person, and wait for the natural pause — never interrupt mid-story; if 20 seconds pass, I move to another group.
   3. IF someone asks "what do you do", THEN I say: "I build AI agents people can run themselves — open source. What brings you here?"
   4. IF nobody talks to me for 10 minutes, THEN I walk to the demo tables/food and ask the nearest person: "Have you seen anything good here?"
   5. IF I want to leave early, THEN I set a minimum of 2 more conversations first, then leave without shame.

9. **Self-distancing / third-person self-talk.** Kross et al. (2014), "Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: how you do it matters", *JPSP* 106(2). Non-anxious participants coached to use non-first-person pronouns ("Eugene, you can do this" / "you" framing) before public speaking showed less rumination, less social anxiety, and *better performance* than "I" self-talk. SINGLE STUDY (high-profile, conceptually consistent with distanced self-talk work). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24467424/ Exact phrasing: talk to yourself by name: "*[Name], you have done this in text for ten years. This is the same thing with mouths.*"

**DEBUNKED:**
- **Power posing.** Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010) claimed expansive poses raised testosterone, lowered cortisol, and changed risk-taking. Failed: Ranehill et al. (2015) found no hormonal effects; Ranehill/Simmons/Simonsohn critiques showed the hormonal and behavioral effects don't replicate; Cuddy herself partially retracted the hormonal claims. What survives: brief expansive posing modestly increases *subjective felt power*. FAILED REPLICATION (hormones), SINGLE/SMALL effect (felt power). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25656333/ Use it only for feeling, not biology — 2 minutes, feet wide, hands on hips, alone in the car. Zero shame if you skip it.
- **Visualizing success only.** Positive outcome visualization alone can reduce motivation and doesn't reduce anxiety (Oettingen's mental contrasting research — fantasizing the outcome alone lowers energization; REPLICATED). Pair visualization of the outcome with visualization of *obstacles and the concrete next action* (WOOP). https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2014-25884-001 (Oettingen's *Rethinking Positive Thinking*, 2014.)
- **"Picture the audience naked"** — no evidence, pure joke advice.
- **Word-for-word scripts** — memorized scripts break on the first deviation and increase anxiety under interruption; use memorized *first sentence only*, everything else is bullet points. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT (consistent with expertise-performance literature on choking).
- **Beta blockers without medical supervision** — they work for performance anxiety but mask tachycardia warnings and have real contraindications; any use needs a physician. Not a hack. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.

**FINAL 10-MINUTE DOOR PROTOCOL:**
1. (10:00) Park. Two physiological sighs. (1 min)
2. (9:00) Inhale 4s / exhale 8s, 6 cycles. (1 min)
3. (8:00) Say out loud, by name: "[Name], you are excited. You have done this for years in text." (1 min)
4. (7:00) Recite your 5 if-thens out loud. (2 min)
5. (5:00) Recite your one 15-second "what I do" sentence and your first opener, out loud, slow, with pauses. (2 min)
6. (3:00) Two more physiological sighs. Drop shoulders. Unclench jaw. (1 min)
7. (2:00) Feet wide, hands loose, walk. Rule: be inside the room and standing near a group within 60 seconds of entry.
8. (0:00) At the group: wait for the pause, make eye contact, first line.

# BLOCK 3 — First 60 seconds and the conversation engine

1. **Opening a closed circle.** Taught mechanics (named practitioners: Leil Lowndes, *How to Talk to Anyone*; Debra Fine, *The Fine Art of Small Talk*; Keith Ferrazzi, *Never Eat Alone*): approach a **group of two, not three+**; approach from the side at ~45°, not head-on; stand slightly outside the circle at the gap between two people's shoulders; make eye contact with one person and wait for the sentence gap; the "triad rule" — a group of 2 open easily, 4+ never will, 3 opens at the moment one person is mid-anecdote. First words: "Mind if I join?" or a topic-relevant comment — the *content matters less than timing*. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.

2. **Question-asking.** Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Gino & Minson (2017), "It doesn't hurt to ask: question-asking increases liking", *JPSP* 113(3). Across speed-dating and lab conversations, people who asked more questions — **specifically more follow-up questions** — were rated more likable; in speed-dating, question-askers got more second dates. Follow-up questions win because they signal listening, and crucially *require no new material from you*. REPLICATED (multi-study series). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28221036/

3. **12 verbatim opening lines (no pitching):**
   1. "Mind if I join you? This is my first one today."
   2. "Have you seen anything good here yet?"
   3. "What are you building?"
   4. "Is this your first hackathon this year, or are you a regular?"
   5. "Which demo should I not miss?"
   6. "I'm deciding between two sessions — what are you here for?"
   7. "That talk/point — what did you think of it?"
   8. "Are you local, or did you fly in for the week?"
   9. "What's the most overrated thing at this event?" (works at hackathons — everyone has opinions)
   10. "Are you here to build, hire, or get hired?" (invites all three of your own goals to surface naturally)
   11. "I heard the line for that was crazy — did you make it in?"
   12. "What's keeping you busy these days?"
   Note the design: every line is a question or an invitation; none is a pitch; #10 lets the *other* person reveal which of your three goals is live.

4. **10 follow-up questions for technical conversations:**
   1. "How are you actually running that — inference on your own boxes or hosted?"
   2. "What did you try before that didn't work?"
   3. "What's the part people get wrong about it?"
   4. "Where does it fall over at scale?"
   5. "Who's using it in production?"
   6. "What would you build if you had a free weekend here?"
   7. "What tooling did you end up writing yourself because nothing fit?"
   8. "What's changed your mind recently?"
   9. "If you were hiring for that team, what would you look for?"
   10. "What should I have asked you that I didn't?"

5. **Names.** What works: repetition at introduction ("Anton — nice to meet you"), immediate use once in the first exchange, and association with one visual/verbal hook. The mechanisms are from memory research (testing effect / spaced retrieval — Roediger & Karpicke 2006, REPLICATED). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16595790/ Procedure on hearing a name: say it back → use it once within 60 seconds ("So, Anton, you were saying…") → write it on your phone *after* the conversation with one fact. In a loud room: lean in, ask "sorry, once more?" — asking twice is normal, not weakness.

6. **15 seconds with THREE goals.** Research says humans process one identity claim at a time and multi-pitches blur; conversation research on self-presentation favors a single memorable claim plus an open loop. UNSOURCED - my judgement layered on Huang 2017. Recommendation: **one identity per conversation — "I build AI agents people can run themselves — open source."** It is true, memorable, and it *bait*: hiring, investment, and co-founder conversations all open from it. If they ask "why SF this week?", *then* say "three reasons: users, a team, and the right lab — but let's start with yours." Pick the goal by *who you're talking to*, not in advance.

7. **Body/voice — supported vs debunked:**
   - Eye contact: moderate, roughly 60–70% of listening time, 3–5 second holds; extreme unbroken staring is rated negatively (Kleinke 1986 review, REPLICATED review). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3521071/
   - Slower speech rate: slower speech with pauses is rated as more competent and, for accented speakers, more intelligible (see Block 1, PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT + phonetics literature).
   - Lower vocal pitch: slight pitch lowering is associated with perceived dominance (Klofstad, Anderson & Peters 2015 on voice pitch and elections; SINGLE STUDY, don't force it — forcing pitch sounds fake).
   - Postural expansion: increases *felt* confidence only (see power posing debunk).
   - Debunked: "take up space to dominate the room", mirroring to build rapport (little support), "alpha body language."

# BLOCK 4 — Hackathons, demos, cameras, speaking slots

1. **Hackathon team formation.** Practitioner advice (hackathon-winning write-ups, MLH community guidance — PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT): first 30 minutes, walk the room, listen for the team whose *problem statement* you'd ship, not the loudest team. Claim a role by stating a deliverable, not a skill level: "I'll own the agent orchestration layer and the demo script by Saturday noon." For weakest-coder/strongest-product profile: state it explicitly — "I'm not the fastest coder in the room; I'm the one who makes sure we have something that runs and a story for the judges." Teams lose to scope creep and demo failure, not to typing speed; the orchestrator role is the highest-leverage seat.

2. **Live-demo failure recovery.** Supported practice: always have a pre-recorded fallback; when it breaks live, **name the bug out loud** ("Classic — the API key expired in the demo env, let me show you the recording and explain what you would have seen"). Narrating the failure keeps the audience in the script, removes dead air, and the beautiful-mess effect (Bruk 2018) says the audience will rate you *better* for handling it. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT on procedure; the likability claim is sourced.

3. **Camera fear (his 2017 block).** Best-supported approach is **graded exposure** (see Block 5 — this is the core evidence base) plus specific tricks:
   - Ladder: voice memo → video with camera off → video camera on, no intention to publish → 1-minute video to one friend → publish a 1-minute clip → publish a 10-minute video → live stream.
   - Talk to ONE person: record as if speaking to a specific named friend; reduces audience-size fear (audience-size is a documented anxiety multiplier — see public-speaking anxiety lit.). UNSOURCED - my judgement.
   - "Second take is the take": do take 1 knowing it's a rehearsal, publish take 2. Removes perfection pressure.
   - Watching your own footage: helps *for specific cues* (rate, filler words) with a checklist; hurts when watched as a self-evaluation without structure (feeds rumination — see Block 5, Clark & Wells post-event processing). Watch once, with notes, then delete or archive. PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT.

4. **Speaker slots.** Giving a talk massively outperforms attending for his goals: a 5-minute slot gives 50–100 people a reason to approach him, inverting the initiation problem. Fear-spike timing: anticipatory anxiety peaks *before* performance and drops sharply once the talk starts (well documented in performance-anxiety research; adrenaline spike then decline). UNSOURCED - my judgement for the curve's exact shape. Say yes to every lightning-talk slot.

5. **Q&A phrasings:**
   - Don't know: "I don't know — here's what I'd guess, and here's how I'd check it."
   - Hostile: "That's a strong claim. What's the strongest version of the evidence behind it?" (never defend, ask for the specifics)
   - Half-understood (accent/noise): "I caught two-thirds of that — was the question about [X] or [Y]?" Always restate before answering.

# BLOCK 5 — Rejection, recovery, keeping the muscle

1. **Exposure therapy, solo.** This is the best-evidenced part: graded exposure with response prevention is the frontline, gold-standard treatment for social anxiety (REPLICATED across dozens of RCTs and meta-analyses; e.g., Mayo-Wilson et al. 2014 CBT meta-analysis for social anxiety disorder). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24842452/ Rules that make it work: (a) stay in the situation until anxiety drops (typically 20–45 min, not 2 min); (b) repeat within 48h — spacing matters; (c) **no safety behaviors** — no phone-hiding, no rehearsed-line-clutching, no alcohol, no standing at the edge; safety behaviors keep the fear alive; (d) drop avoidance: attend, don't escape.
   **The 10-step ladder:** 1. Eye contact + "thanks, have a good one" to a barista. 2. Ask a stranger for the time. 3. Ask a hotel employee one real question ("where do locals eat?"). 4. One compliment to a stranger. 5. Ask one question at a meetup Q&A mic. 6. Initiate one conversation with a stranger at an event. 7. Join a closed circle of 3. 8. Ask one event stranger for a coffee/15-minute follow-up. 9. Record a 1-minute video and publish. 10. Give a lightning talk.
   Each step: repeat until Subjective Units of Distress drop by half before moving up.

2. **Rejection therapy (Jia Jiang).** Claim: deliberately seeking rejection 100 days desensitizes fear of it. Evidence status: single-person case, popularized by TEDx; the underlying principle (repeated exposure to refusal) is valid exposure logic, REPLICATED as a *mechanism*; the program itself is UNSOURCED as an intervention. Sane version: seek one small rejection per week (ask for something unreasonable politely), log the outcome, note that the predicted catastrophe never arrives.

3. **Post-event crash.** The Clark & Wells (1995) model of social anxiety: after an event, anxious people replay it using distorted internal images and feelings as "evidence" of failure — the post-event rumination loop, which maintains the anxiety. Treatment: review against external facts, not feelings. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9780470973799.ch1 (chapter; also Clark & Wells 1995 in *Advances in Behaviour Therapy*). What breaks the loop: (1) a factual scorecard (below) written within 2 hours; (2) an externally anchored question — "what did they actually *say* back?" (they answered = it went fine); (3) scheduled worry time (30 minutes, then stop); (4) physical activity. Avoid alcohol as the post-event sedative (rebound anxiety).

4. **Scorecard — count attempts, not outcomes.** Process goals beat outcome goals in performance psychology (Burton et al. on goal orientation; PRACTITIONER-adjacent, the general finding is REPLICATED in sport psych). His card: conversations initiated (target 10), follow-up questions asked (target 25), questions asked at mic (1), name remember attempts (all), rejections collected (target 2 — collecting rejections is the *win* for desensitization). Outcomes (cards, interviews, commits) are logged separately, monthly, and not judged daily.

5. **Energy — 2 events/week for months.** Anxious people pay a higher arousal cost per event, so budget like an athlete: hard event nights are followed by a protected low-stimulus morning; one full day per week with zero social performance; the 4s/8s breathing before *and* a physiological sigh after each event; no more than 2 networking events back-to-back days; treat car time as decompression (audio, no calls). PRACTITIONER JUDGEMENT — the cost asymmetry is real, the schedule is my construction.

6. **One-year view.** Exposure-based change is dose-dependent: in treatment trials, meaningful reduction typically appears within 8–15 exposure sessions, and continuous in-vivo practice (weekly, unescaped) compounds for 6–12 months. The honest number from CBT trials: most social-anxiety symptom reduction occurs over roughly 12–16 weekly sessions of practice with no avoidance. UNSOURCED - my judgement on the numbers (session counts across trials vary; the direction is REPLICATED). For him: ~50 events ≈ the rough equivalent of a treatment course.

## B. THE WALLET CARD (at the door)

1. Sigh twice. In 4, out 8 — six rounds.
2. Say it: "[Name], you are EXCITED."
3. Join a group within 60 seconds. Two people, not four.
4. Wait for the pause. Eye contact. "Mind if I join?"
5. Ask. Follow up. Ask again. They talk; you win.
6. Slow down 15%. Pause every sentence. Accent is noise, not signal.
7. "I build AI agents people can run themselves — open source." One identity per conversation.
8. Don't know? "I don't know — here's how I'd check."
9. Awkward silence = their data, not yours. They liked it more than you feel.
10. Two more conversations before you leave. Then leave — no shame.
11. Score attempts tonight, not outcomes. Rejections count as points.
12. Write the card, breathe, sleep. Re-run within 48 hours.

## C. IF I ONLY HAD 3 THINGS

1. **Daily/weekly exposure ladder with no safety behaviors and 48-hour repeat spacing.** It's the only thing with treatment-grade evidence for permanently lowering the baseline. One perfect conversation technique is worth nothing if he never walks in.
2. **The if-then plans + door protocol (breathing + "I am excited" + named self-talk).** They attack the exact failure point he documents: courage exists, execution fails "in the concrete moment." If-thens automatize the moment; that's their measured job.
3. **Question-asking as the conversation engine.** It converts his non-native-speaker cost into a strength — follow-up questions mean *the other person* does the talking, his accent friction drops by as much as his own speech drops, and the single best-sourced social-psych finding (Huang 2017) says it's also what makes people like him.
Not chosen: power posing (dead), "be confident" (nothing), memorized scripts (fragile), even the opening lines (content is the least important variable — timing and repetition are what matter).

## D. WHERE I MIGHT BE WRONG

1. **The 8–15-session / ~50-event habituation numbers.** Derived from treatment trials that include therapist structure; self-directed exposure shows weaker and slower effects. If a proper meta-analysis of *self-guided* social exposure showed dropout > 50% and small effects, my one-year projections are too optimistic.
2. **"One identity per conversation."** My own UNSOURCED synthesis — a multi-goal "portfolio pitch" might test better in founder populations specifically (no study I know of tests this directly). A/B testing his own 2 weeks of events would settle it.
3. **The accent-rate advice.** I asserted slowed speech + front-loaded first sentence as friction-reducers; the phonetics literature on accented-speech intelligibility is messier than my confident framing, and some listeners read slow speech as low status. If intelligibility-vs-status trade-off studies showed fast-but-clear beats slow, the pacing advice should be revised.
