# Telegram as a Transport Rail for a Multi-Machine Autonomous Agent Fleet

## Executive Summary

Telegram is viable as a **human-readable coordination surface** for a tiny, low-volume agent fleet, but it is a weak choice for the **authoritative transport rail** of a production consensus system. The core reasons are not ideological; they are mechanical: Telegram does not give you broker-grade replay, durable acknowledgements, stable machine-oriented ordering guarantees, or immutability; the Bot API is explicitly update-driven and rate-limited, and MTProto userbot usage sits behind opaque anti-abuse heuristics and account-risk policies. Telegram is therefore good at **transparency and operator visibility**, but poor as the single source of truth for **machine-to-machine correctness**. citeturn37view1turn37view2turn29view3turn38view0turn28view3

For your exact topology—five to six machines, low message volume, human approval gates, and a VPS you already operate—the best current architecture is a **hybrid**: move the consensus engine’s primary event transport to a small self-hosted broker on the VPS, and keep Telegram as a **readable mirror plus human command ingress**. Of the alternatives, **NATS with JetStream** is the cleanest fit because it gives durable replay, subject-based routing, lightweight ops, and straightforward support for “observer/mirror” patterns; it also already appears in recent multi-agent orchestration work as a backbone combined with persistence and a passive dashboard layer. citeturn28view6turn29view7turn29view8turn31view7

The strongest immediate intervention is not even the broker migration. It is this: **stop letting multiple machines directly hold or reuse the same Telegram MTProto session**. Telegram’s own error semantics say `AUTH_KEY_DUPLICATED` invalidates the session and requires a fresh login, while Telethon explicitly warns that third-party-library usage can trigger suspicious-activity patterns that get accounts limited. A single Telegram gateway process on the VPS, with every node talking to that gateway over a broker or signed HTTPS, removes your current highest-probability operational footgun in one move. citeturn29view5turn28view3turn29view6

The deeper design lesson matches the broader industry trajectory from ChatOps toward GitOps and broker-backed control planes: chat remains useful for **invocation, collaboration, visibility, and incident coordination**, but authoritative state increasingly lives somewhere else—Git, an incident system, or a message bus. Argo CD openly treats Git as source of truth and sends notifications outward to Slack; Atlassian ChatOps automates incident rooms but ties them to Jira-managed incident state. ## Key Findings

Telegram’s official Bot API is a poor primary rail for multi-agent consensus in groups. Bots do **not** see messages from other bots in groups, regardless of privacy mode, specifically to avoid loops. Updates arrive through long polling or webhooks, and `getUpdates` is fundamentally a queue interface with at most 100 returned updates per call, not a general-purpose durable history interface. That alone makes a multi-bot consensus topology brittle for offline catch-up and cross-bot visibility. citeturn37view1turn37view2turn28view5

Telegram’s documented hard limits are also misaligned with even modest automation bursts. The Bot API caps text messages at **1–4096 characters** after entity parsing. Telegram also documents practical sending limits of about **one message per second in a single chat**, **20 messages per minute in a group**, and roughly **30 broadcasts per second** unless “paid broadcasts” are enabled. Those are not fatal for your current traffic, but they are low enough that mirroring every consensus sub-event, verification event, retry, and human annotation can consume margin quickly. citeturn34view0turn36view0turn36view1turn36view2

MTProto userbots remove some Bot API restrictions, but they replace documented limits with ambiguous anti-abuse behavior. Telethon’s FAQ states that third-party libraries are easier to misuse and that Telegram learns suspicious patterns from abusive usage; Telethon also documents a concrete flood heuristic for history retrieval—roughly **30 seconds per 10 `GetHistoryRequest`s**. Official Telegram documentation further states that if an `AUTH_KEY_DUPLICATED` error occurs, the session has already been invalidated and must be re-created. In practice, that means a shared session string across multiple IPs is not just fragile; it is structurally unstable. citeturn28view3turn29view0turn29view5

Telegram’s data model is also unsuitable as a consensus ledger. Message IDs order messages within a chat, and editing a message does **not** change its ID; deletes and edits therefore preserve referenceability but do not give you immutability. Group semantics are also messy: basic groups share a message-ID/PTS space with private chats, while migrating a basic group to a supergroup creates a new destination and requires clients to merge history from both old and new locations client-side. If you insist on Telegram transport at all, use a **private supergroup only**, never a basic group, and never treat Telegram message order as protocol truth. citeturn29view3turn35view1turn35view0

From the reliability literature, your intuition that consensus should not depend on a flaky or third-party channel is well supported. Work comparing BFT protocols under realistic WAN delay, bandwidth reduction, and packet loss found materially different behavior once protocols leave “clean LAN” assumptions. XFT argues that classical crash-only and Byzantine-only models miss a large practical middle ground where network faults and machine faults interact. PeerReview shows why a **tamper-evident accountability layer** is valuable even when you do not deploy full Byzantine machinery. Recent work on cross-cluster RSM communication emphasizes explicit communication primitives and acknowledgement schemes, not ad hoc chat rails. citeturn31view0turn31view1turn31view2turn31view3

The industry pattern is not “chat control planes disappeared.” It is more specific: **chat stayed, but authority moved out of chat**. GitHub still uses ChatOps for on-call and workflow triggers. Atlassian uses ChatOps for incident coordination. But GitOps systems such as Argo CD explicitly make **Git the source of truth** and use Slack only as an outward notification sink. This is the strongest available real-world analogue to your problem, and it points toward a broker-plus-chat-mirror architecture, not Telegram-as-ledger. citeturn6search0turn6search5turn29view14turn29view15turn29view16

Emerging agent standards reinforce that direction. **A2A** is converging on HTTP(S) + JSON-RPC + SSE, discovery via **Agent Cards**, and explicit task lifecycle management. **MCP** is converging on standardized tool/context connections, with **Tasks** for durable handles and pollable long-running operations. Neither standard says “use chat as your transport.” Both assume agent interoperability over explicit web/event interfaces, with structured capabilities and durable task state. **CloudEvents** gives you a portable event envelope today. citeturn28view10turn30search0turn30search1turn30search3turn28view11turn29view19turn29view20turn28view12

## Contradictions & Debates

There is a real debate between **transparency** and **transport purity**, and your current design is intelligently biased toward transparency. Chat-native coordination is operationally valuable because humans can inspect negotiation in the same space where machines coordinate; GitHub’s ChatOps framing and Atlassian’s incident workflow both validate that benefit. The mistake is not wanting a readable channel. The mistake is conflating **readability** with **reliability**. Mature systems increasingly preserve the human-readable mirror while moving authoritative state into a durable substrate. citeturn6search0turn6search5turn29view14turn29view15

A second debate is whether a tiny fleet really needs a “real broker.” Strictly speaking, not always. For five to six nodes and very low event rates, **plain signed HTTPS webhooks plus a durable database** can work if you are willing to build replay, fan-out, retry, and observability yourself. The question is not scale in the abstract; it is whether you want to keep paying custom-complexity tax around ordering, anti-entropy, dedupe, and operator tooling. Because JetStream already gives store-and-replay semantics, and because you already run a VPS, the broker is not overengineering here; it is probably the simpler system after the first week. citeturn28view6turn29view12turn29view13

A third debate is whether to keep MTProto userbots or move to the Bot API. For a **Telegram mirror**, the Bot API is the safer operational choice because it is documented, webhook-friendly, and officially designed for automation. For a **Telegram-ingested machine bus**, Bot API is blocked by the “bots cannot see other bots” rule and limited update queue semantics. MTProto userbots retain more visibility, but they carry significantly more account risk and much fuzzier guardrails. That is why the right answer is not “switch everything to Bot API.” The right answer is “stop using Telegram as the machine bus, then use Bot API for the mirror.” citeturn37view1turn37view2turn28view5turn28view3

Finally, there is a live debate in the research world between “small-fleet BFT everywhere” and “lighter accountability plus signatures plus human gates.” For your setup, full BFT is probably not the highest-leverage next step. The literature supports careful leader selection, adaptive weighting, accountable logs, and explicit tamper evidence as meaningful practical gains in small, heterogeneous fleets. Given your irreversible-action approval gate, **signed, append-only, accountable eventing** is likely higher value than trying to force a heavy Byzantine protocol through Telegram semantics. citeturn31view1turn31view2turn31view6

## Best Practices

The first best practice is architectural: separate **transport**, **ledger**, and **human mirror**. The consensus engine should emit one canonical structured event to a rail-agnostic envelope. A transport adapter should deliver that event to NATS, HTTPS, email, Telegram, or anything else. A mirror adapter should render a concise human-readable summary into Telegram. The Telegram post should be treated as a **projection** of the event, not the event itself. This directly follows the way CloudEvents standardizes envelopes and the way GitOps systems externalize notifications away from the authoritative substrate. citeturn28view12turn29view14turn29view15

The second best practice is to make every event **cryptographically accountable**. PeerReview’s lesson still applies: strong node identities, tamper-evident records, and non-repudiable actions are extremely effective in mixed-trust or distributed-admin systems. In your case that means enforcing Ed25519 signatures now, not later, and signing a deterministic canonical payload that includes at minimum `event_id`, `node_id`, `phase`, `risk_tier`, `time`, `local_seq`, `prev_hash`, `payload_hash`, and `requires_human_approval`. If you adopt CloudEvents, put these as extension attributes rather than inventing a completely bespoke wrapper. citeturn31view2turn28view12

The third best practice is to implement **application-level ordering** instead of inferring order from Telegram. Use one of: a per-node monotonic sequence plus Lamport clock, or a broker-issued durable sequence plus parent hash chain. Telegram message ordering is insufficient once edits, deletes, migrations, retries, and multi-rail anti-entropy appear. NATS ordered consumers, Redis stream IDs, and MQTT’s per-topic ordering behavior all underline the same point: ordering has to be designed explicitly and scoped clearly. citeturn29view3turn29view7turn28view7turn29view9

The fourth best practice is to centralize all Telegram interactions in a **single gateway**. Do not let every machine speak MTProto. Give the VPN/VPS-hosted gateway sole custody of the Telegram account or bot token, enforce rate limiting there, and let nodes communicate with the gateway over broker subjects or signed HTTPS. Telegram itself supports multiple authorization keys in principle, but duplicated keys invalidate the session; centralization removes that risk and reduces the chance of looking like a botnet or a Telegram-based C2 cluster. citeturn29view6turn29view5turn38view0

The fifth best practice is to keep Telegram content **redacted by default**. Telegram cloud chats are not an immutable, self-hosted, cryptographically auditable ledger, and bot communications use ordinary HTTPS rather than MTProto. Post human-readable summaries, hashes, compact JSON stubs, and broker references. Put sensitive payload bodies, full artifacts, and irreversible-action parameters in the broker/store. This also helps you avoid looking like the common malware pattern of shipping archives, screenshots, or structured exfil blobs through Telegram APIs. citeturn16news37turn15search0turn16search15turn38view0

A final best practice is anti-abuse hygiene. Telegram’s own moderation stack collects metadata such as IP address, device/app history, and username changes for abuse prevention, and it may use automated algorithms to analyze cloud-chat messages. Accounts are limited when spam reports are confirmed, and repeated unsolicited messaging can permanently remove the ability to contact strangers. Legitimate automation should therefore stay inside a **single private supergroup**, avoid cold DMs, avoid invite-link spraying, avoid “marketing-like” or repetitive broadcast text, avoid endpoint-direct uploads of archives/screenshots, and keep a stable, long-lived identity with 2FA. citeturn38view0turn39view2turn39view4

## Failure Modes

The highest-probability failure mode in your current design is **session invalidation**. Telegram’s official error handling states that `AUTH_KEY_DUPLICATED` means the session was invalidated and must be re-created. If a session leaks or is reused from multiple locations, the transport can fail catastrophically at the authentication layer, not just the message layer. Recovery is operationally annoying because it requires regenerating the auth key and logging in again. citeturn29view5

The second failure mode is **anti-spam limitation or ban**. Telegram limits accounts after user reports are confirmed by moderators; repeated offenses increase the duration, and continued unwanted messaging can permanently remove the ability to contact strangers. Telegram explicitly sends wrongfully limited users to `@SpamBot`, but it also says early release for actually abusive behavior is impossible. For legitimate systems, that means recovery is possible in some cases, but it is not something you should plan around as an SLO-bearing mechanism. citeturn39view2turn39view1

The third failure mode is **platform-level policy shift**. Since late 2024 Telegram has been tightening moderation posture, removing abused features, updating privacy policy for broader lawful disclosure, and adding anti-scam features such as third-party verification. That does not mean legitimate private-group automation is forbidden. It does mean the platform has become more attentive to abuse patterns and more motivated to reduce its “criminal infrastructure” reputation. A system that resembles structured C2 traffic therefore faces more policy risk now than it did a few years ago. citeturn27search9turn38view2turn38view3turn38view0

The fourth failure mode is **semantic corruption through mutability**. Telegram messages can be edited, and bots can delete many messages within 48 hours. Even when edits preserve message IDs, they alter the visible record. That is acceptable for a mirror, unacceptable for a ledger. If humans can clean up, correct, or remove machine messages in the same chat, then Telegram history is not a forensically reliable consensus transcript without an external immutable archive. citeturn29view3turn29view4

The fifth failure mode is **migration and catch-up ambiguity**. If a basic group is migrated to a supergroup, clients must merge history from both sides. On the Bot API side, updates are just a queue with pending counts and confirmation offsets; there is no official arbitrary-history read primitive exposed in the Bot API docs. That creates fragile edge cases for offline nodes, delayed consumers, and replay after bridge outages. citeturn35view0turn35view1turn37view2

The sixth failure mode is **regional or geopolitical outage**. Telegram has suffered service disruptions and regional restrictions, including DDoS-related access issues in Russia in 2024, region-specific blocking in 2025, and additional Russian restriction pressure in 2026. Even if your fleet is geographically distributed, relying on a third-party public chat platform adds an external dependency you cannot route around on your own terms. citeturn12news25turn13news37turn13news39

## Strategic Recommendations

The concrete verdict for your case is: **do not fully stay on Telegram as the primary transport**. Harden it immediately for continuity, but treat that as temporary containment. The strategically correct destination is a **hybrid architecture** in which a self-hosted broker is authoritative and Telegram is a human-readable mirror plus optional human command surface. If you want the shortest path with the largest reliability gain, use **NATS + JetStream** on the VPS. It is light enough for one VPS, gives built-in message persistence and replay, and supports ordered-consumer patterns useful for observers, dashboards, and repair tools. citeturn28view6turn29view7turn29view8

If you need a one-line decision: **migrate consensus events to NATS/JetStream, keep Telegram as mirror, and run exactly one Telegram gateway process on the VPS**. That preserves the human-readable property you consider load-bearing while removing Telegram from the critical correctness path. It also matches recent edge/multi-agent patterns where NATS backs durable eventing and a passive observer or dashboard mirrors the traffic for humans without becoming part of delivery itself. citeturn31view7

## Action Plan

**Immediate containment**

1. Move Telegram access behind a **single VPS-hosted gateway**. No machine except the gateway should hold the Telegram session or bot token. This removes the `AUTH_KEY_DUPLICATED` failure class from the fleet path. citeturn29view5turn29view6  
2. Enforce **Ed25519 signatures** on every event now. Do not leave this as “planned.” The signature should cover deterministic canonical JSON. citeturn31view2turn28view12  
3. Freeze protocol mutability: **no edits as state changes**. Corrections become new events referencing prior event IDs. Telegram edits remain allowed only for cosmetic mirror formatting, never ledger semantics. citeturn29view3turn29view4  
4. Restrict Telegram content to **summary + compact JSON stub + hash/reference**. Do not place secrets or full irreversible payloads into the chat. citeturn16news37turn38view0  

**Primary transport migration**

5. Stand up **NATS with JetStream** on the VPS. Create subjects such as `fleet.consensus.propose`, `fleet.consensus.respond`, `fleet.consensus.commit`, `fleet.consensus.verify`, and `fleet.system.heartbeat`. Store them in durable streams with retention sized for at least weeks of replay. citeturn28view6turn29view7  
6. Introduce a rail-agnostic event envelope, ideally aligned with **CloudEvents** plus consensus-specific extension fields. This gives you transport portability into NATS, HTTPS, Git, email, or Telegram mirrors. citeturn28view12  
7. Add a **passive observer/mirror service** that subscribes to broker subjects, writes an immutable local archive, and posts summarized projections to Telegram. Architecturally, this should resemble the passive-observer pattern in EdgeCitadel rather than sitting inline with machine delivery. citeturn31view7  

**Operator and safety model**

8. Add explicit **heartbeat/presence** to the broker, not to Telegram polling. Presence belongs in machine transport. Telegram should display it, not define it. If you later adopt A2A/MCP-style tasking, this maps naturally onto durable task handles and status updates. citeturn29view19turn29view20turn30search0  
9. Keep human approval where it is, but make approval a **signed event on the broker**, with Telegram merely showing the approval request and result. citeturn20search0turn28view12  
10. Add periodic anti-entropy from the broker archive to secondary stores such as Git or Drive if you want belt-and-suspenders recovery. The broker should still remain the live source of truth.

**Telegram-specific hygiene while the mirror remains**

11. Keep the transport inside one **private supergroup** only. Avoid basic groups and unnecessary migrations. citeturn35view0turn35view1  
12. Avoid cold DMs, invite-link spraying, advertising-like text, and endpoint-originated structured uploads that resemble common Telegram C2 or exfil use.
