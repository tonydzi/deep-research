# Distributed ON AIR Board for a Small AI Fleet

## Findings at a glance

The right design for your fleet is **not** a distributed lock service. It is a **coarse-grained, advisory declaration board** that sits above your existing fine-grained file lease and below the after-the-fact journal: a visible statement of intent for “I am doing structural work in zone X now,” with a holder identity, mode, TTL, and heartbeat. That is exactly how mature systems separate concerns: LOTO uses visible tags plus worker identity and removal rules; aviation separates “intent to operate” from “temporary hazard notices”; Chubby explicitly argues for **coarse** leases for large structural coordination; GitHub/GitLab use assignees and draft/WIP states to advertise ownership without pretending to provide serializable exclusivity. citeturn8view1turn8view2turn30view2turn34view0turn28view0turn28view2turn24search2turn14search2

The hard boundary is this: with **Syncthing as an eventually consistent file sync** and **no strongly consistent coordination store**, your board can reduce collisions, speed up human deconfliction, and recover from crashed holders via TTLs, but it **cannot guarantee single-writer exclusivity** during network partitions or stale reads. The consistent systems you cited as precedents—Chubby, ZooKeeper, and Kubernetes Leases—work because they sit on a coordination substrate that can decide liveness and order updates consistently; Syncthing instead resolves concurrent file edits by creating conflict copies, at file granularity, after the fact. Even kernel-enforced “mandatory locking” is documented by Linux as unreliable due to race conditions; trying to fake mandatory semantics over synced files is a dead end. citeturn28view0turn28view2turn27view2turn12view0turn36search0turn27view1

So the correct target is: **honest advisory coordination**. Make the board cheap to declare, impossible to miss, easy to age out, and explicit about who currently “has the conn” for a subsystem. That is the minimum design most likely to survive both machine crashes and human laziness. citeturn31view3turn33view3turn25search1

## Precedents worth stealing

**Industrial safety gives you the strongest semantics vocabulary.** OSHA’s lockout/tagout rule requires that tags be **identifiable**, that affected employees be **notified** before application and after removal, that each device normally be removed by the employee who applied it, and that group lockout assign **primary responsibility** to an authorized employee while still requiring each worker to affix and remove their own personal lock. OSHA also requires explicit procedures for **shift or personnel changes** so protection is transferred in an orderly way, plus annual inspection, retraining, and documentation. CCOHS puts the cultural point bluntly: lockout/tagout is not just hardware; it is a process of **communication, coordination, and training**. **Aviation gives you the advance-intent pattern and the stale-notice lesson.** FAA flight-plan handling is built around recording, filing, modifying, canceling, activating, and closing flight plans for **appropriate distribution and processing**. NOTAMs are the separate channel for temporary changes or hazards in the airspace system. ## Failure modes and proven mitigations

**Stale claims.** These are the open-source “issue squatting” failure and the aviation “old NOTAM” failure in different clothes: work was real once, but nobody cleaned it up, so the board stops reflecting reality. The mitigation is a **lease with expiry**, a two-stage freshness model (**active → stale/expiring → expired**), and a requirement to renew or let it lapse. The enforcement should be mostly automation, with a grace warning rather than hard deletion first. citeturn35view0turn32view0turn32view1

**Crashed holders.** This is what leases were invented for. ZooKeeper solves it with session expiration and ephemeral nodes; Kubernetes records `renewTime`; DHCP clients must stop using an address once the lease expires if they cannot renew. For your board, the mitigation is simple: the holder writes a heartbeat periodically, clean exits close early, and readers treat expired declarations as non-blocking after a grace window. Automation should enforce expiry; humans should not need to manually “remove the dead lock.” citeturn27view2turn12view0turn26view2

**Clock skew plus eventual consistency.** With long-lived declarations, moderate clock skew is manageable; with short TTLs it becomes toxic. Kubernetes leader election explicitly warns that lease timing must fit your clock-skew tolerance. Syncthing adds propagation lag and file-level race resolution, so a declaration board on top of Syncthing should use **hours, not minutes**, for TTLs. The mitigation is long TTLs relative to skew/lag, conservative expiry, and no sub-minute semantics. Automation enforces the timing; the norm is “do not infer precision from an eventually consistent channel.” citeturn11search0turn11search17turn36search0

**Over-claiming.** Teams over-broaden scope because it feels safer (“I own canon”, “I own sync”), but that quickly becomes pseudo-freeze and people route around the board. Chubby’s case for coarse locks is not a case for **maximally broad** locks; it is a case for locks sized to the coordination problem. The mitigation is a **small zone taxonomy with parent/child relations**, a requirement to choose the narrowest zone that matches the work, and a default expectation that cross-cutting work names primary plus a short `also_touches` list instead of claiming the entire world. This is mostly a norm reinforced by lightweight linting. citeturn28view2turn23view0

**Under-claiming.** If declaring is annoying, agents and humans will skip it. LOTO works culturally because the tag is part of the job, not an optional afterthought. GitHub draft PRs and issue assignment also work because creation is low-friction and fits the natural workflow. The mitigation is to make declaration nearly zero-cost and to hook it into startup, pre-edit, and scheduled-job wrappers so “declare or explicitly skip” is the default path. Enforcement should be automation at workflow choke points, backed by a cultural rule that structural work without a declaration is process debt. citeturn31view3turn24search0turn24search2

**Boards nobody reads.** OSHA requires tags at the isolating device and immediately obvious to anyone trying to operate it. Google SRE’s live incident document works because it is the working surface during the incident, not a forgotten dashboard. The mitigation is the digital equivalent of “on the door”: show the active declarations at **session start**, before **risky edits**, and in the **Telegram rail** where operators already pay attention. Enforcement is mostly automation and UI placement, not policy prose. citeturn8view1turn33view3turn25search1

**Alert fatigue.** If every heartbeat pings Telegram, the rail becomes wallpaper. Aviation learned that temporary notices lose value when the channel is overloaded, and GitHub stale bots use **warning, then action**, not constant noise. The mitigation is to message only on **create, mode change, significant extension, explicit close, conflict detection, or expiry**, while keeping heartbeats silent in the files. Enforcement is automation. citeturn30view1turn30view0turn32view0

**Deadlock between two exclusive claims.** This is the classic “I need your zone; you need mine” standoff. LOTO handles multi-worker coordination by assigning a primary responsible person; incident management handles it with a clearly named commander. The mitigation is an **escalation ladder**: first direct contact with the holder, then explicit decision by a designated tie-break authority, not endless mutual waiting. Enforcement is a mix: automation detects the intersection; humans resolve priority. citeturn8view1turn33view3turn25search5

**Split brain during sync outage.** This is the irreducible failure mode in your environment: two sides can both legitimately believe the zone is free and publish overlapping claims while disconnected. Syncthing will reconcile files later, but that does not retroactively prevent concurrent work. The mitigation is not “better exclusivity”; it is **post-reconnect conflict detection, deterministic loser election, and fast human reconciliation**. This must be enforced partly by automation and partly by norm, because no advisory board on an eventually consistent substrate can do more. citeturn36search0turn36search1turn27view2turn12view0

## Minimal robust design for your fleet

The simplest durable source of truth is **one flat file per declaration**, placed in a shared `onair/active/` directory, with a derived human summary file such as `onair/ACTIVE_NOW.md` generated locally from those declarations. Do **not** use one mutable ledger file as the source of truth. Under Syncthing, concurrent edits are resolved at the **file** level, creating conflict copies; one-file-per-declaration localizes conflicts to the holder’s record instead of turning the whole board into a merge hotspot. This recommendation is an inference from Syncthing’s conflict behavior rather than an explicit Syncthing prescription, but it is the right inference for your topology. citeturn36search0turn36search1

A minimal declaration record should have immutable identity, a zone, a mode, a holder block, timestamps, a lease duration, and links back to the live conversation or work artifact. Keep the holder as the **only normal writer** to the file; other sessions read it, but they do not edit or delete it. That follows both LOTO’s “normally the applier removes it” rule and the general lease pattern in Kubernetes/ZooKeeper, where ownership changes happen by expiry or explicit release, not by casual third-party mutation. citeturn8view1turn27view2turn12view0

A few schema rules matter more than the exact syntax. Use **derived expiry** (`renewed_at + ttl_sec`) instead of storing a second `expires_at`, because duplicate time fields drift. Keep `status` small—`active` and `closed` are enough—and derive display states such as `expiring`, `stale`, or `expired` in the board renderer. Retain closed or expired declarations in `onair/archive/` for audit, but never require someone to merge archival state into the active record. Those are simple data-hygiene rules, but they are exactly the kind that keep coordination services legible over time. citeturn12view0turn32view0turn33view3

Your current three-mode proposal is the right minimum: **EXCLUSIVE**, **COLLAB-talk-first**, and **FYI**. Two modes are too coarse because they collapse “do not touch” and “touch only after coordination,” which are materially different behaviors. Four modes are possible, but they usually create taxonomy debt without much gain at your scale. If you need a stronger notion later, add a **board-wide maintenance freeze** as a separate global flag, not as a fourth per-declaration mode. That matches the way practice separates hard gates like deploy freezes from softer ownership markers like draft PRs or assignees. citeturn33view1turn24search2turn14search2

Semantically, I would define the modes this way. **EXCLUSIVE** means “no overlapping structural edits in this zone or its descendants without explicit release or override.” **COLLAB-talk-first** means “reading is fine, but risky edits require an explicit acknowledgement in Telegram or a direct handoff.” **FYI** means “I am actively changing this area; you may proceed, but you should inspect the linked branch/journal first.” This keeps the board advisory, but with enough sharpness that agents can behave differently by mode rather than treating everything as a vague warning. citeturn8view1turn24search2turn25search1

For TTLs, steal the **lease-renew-before-expiry** pattern, but scale it to your environment instead of copying cluster defaults. A good baseline is: **interactive structural work gets `ttl_sec = 12h`**, **headless long-running work gets `ttl_sec = 24h`**, and **heartbeat interval = min(3h, TTL/4)**, plus renew on resume/wake and close immediately on clean exit. For multi-day work, do **daily renewal** of a 24-hour claim rather than issuing a 72-hour claim. This follows the logic of DHCP’s staged renewal and Kubernetes’ distinction between clean release and TTL expiry, while avoiding zombie claims and renewal spam. With Syncthing lag measured in seconds-to-minutes, hour-scale heartbeats are conservative enough that skew and propagation do not dominate. citeturn26view0turn26view1turn12view0turn11search0turn36search0

The intersection rule should be deterministic and boring. Two declarations intersect if their `zone` values are equal, or if one is a parent prefix of the other, or if any `also_touches` entry intersects the other declaration’s primary zone. Keep the zone taxonomy small—roughly **8 to 15 durable zones**, with optional subzones by path prefix—because blackboard/repository systems become unreadable when the control space explodes. This is where many small-team systems accidentally die: not from lack of capability, but from too many subtly overlapping names. citeturn23view0turn23view1

When a new declaration intersects an existing one, use an **advisory escalation ladder** rather than pretending to have atomic lock acquisition. If it intersects **FYI**, proceed and include the link in the new declaration. If it intersects **COLLAB**, announce intent in Telegram and require explicit acknowledgement before risky edits. If it intersects **EXCLUSIVE**, do not start structural edits; page the holder; if the declaration is expired, wait a short grace period and then proceed with a new declaration that references the expired one. In practice, that can be as simple as an ordered priority list such as `vps > founder-laptop > others`, with human override always allowed. This preserves the “repairable by a non-technical owner” property because the leader does not need to be up for the board to work; it only reduces ambiguity when split brain is discovered after the fact. citeturn34view1turn33view3

To make the board actually read, place it where action happens.
