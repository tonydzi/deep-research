# Insight (DR DR26-08-29-MACANTON-01-0742): Meta: reusable deep-research methodology template, applied to urban heat islands & public health in EU cities

> Since the original DR brief had no specified topic, the report delivers a reusable deep-research workflow template and demonstrates it on urban heat islands (UHI) and public health in European cities.

## Ключевые выводы
- Europe recorded an estimated 61,672 heat-related deaths in summer 2022 and 47,690 in 2023; the 2023 estimate implies deaths would have been ~80% higher without present-century adaptation.
- In an 85-city, 500m-resolution study (Nature Communications), UHI was associated with a median 45% increase in mortality risk during heat-extreme days, costing ~€192 per adult inhabitant/year on average.
- A 93-city Lancet study found cities averaged 1.5°C hotter than surrounding rural areas in summer 2015, causing 6,700 premature deaths (4.3% of summer mortality); raising tree cover to 30% could have prevented ~2,644 of those deaths.
- Across 854 European cities (2000-2019), annual excess mortality from non-optimal temperatures was 20,173 heat-attributable vs. 203,620 cold-attributable deaths (heat is a smaller but rising share).
- Exposure is geographically concentrated: near-total UHI exposure in Malaga, Genoa, Athens; highest absolute exposed populations in Paris, Athens, Barcelona; nearly half of hospitals/schools in EU cities sit in zones with UHI intensity above 2°C.
- Typical EU city UHI intensity is 4-6°C on average with peaks up to 10°C (JRC); Antwerp's city centre averages ~2°C UHI yearly, up to 9°C on summer evenings/nights.
- Main mechanisms: sealed/impervious surfaces, low vegetation, narrow streets blocking airflow, anthropogenic heat, and nighttime heat retention.
- Best-supported interventions are portfolios, not single fixes: heat-health warning systems, targeted outreach to vulnerable groups, greening/shade/cool surfaces, and health-system readiness (WHO's 8-element HHAP framework); city examples include Antwerp's mandatory green roofs, Brussels' cooling islands, Barcelona's cooling shelters, Murcia's reflective paving.
- Evidence is stronger for mortality than morbidity, for outdoor/neighbourhood exposure than indoor/nighttime exposure, and for modelled intervention scenarios than measured post-implementation outcomes; EEA notes inconsistent cross-country surveillance limits near-real-time EU health assessment.

## Рекомендации / решения
- Use a fixed 6-step deep-research workflow for any topic: (1) define problem scope with 5 explicit questions, (2) build a source hierarchy (peer-reviewed > multilateral/national bodies > official datasets > case studies), (3) apply explicit inclusion/exclusion screening rules, (4) extract data into a standardized matrix (citation, geography, period, metrics, method, limitations, policy relevance), (5) grade evidence strength (high/moderate/exploratory), (6) predefine report output structure.
- For any applied report, structure the final deliverable as: executive summary, methods, evidence table, quantitative synthesis, heterogeneity/limitations, policy section, references — plus one causal diagram, one comparison table, one outcome chart.
- When researching UHI/heat-health topics specifically, prioritize four evidence layers: policy/surveillance (WHO, EEA), exposure/urban climate (Copernicus/UrbClim, Climate-ADAPT), health-burden (multicity epidemiology in Nature Communications/Lancet/Lancet Planetary Health/Nature Medicine), and implementation (municipal case studies).
- Any serious city-heat report should include an environmental-justice/equity lens (uneven exposure by city and within cities), not just citywide averages.
- Distinguish established evidence from plausible-but-unvalidated intervention claims explicitly, given the gap between modelled and measured outcomes.
